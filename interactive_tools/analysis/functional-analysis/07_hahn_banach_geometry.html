7

Hahn–Banach: there is always a hyperplane

A functional known only on a subspace can be extended to the whole space without growing. Geometrically, a line that touches the unit ball can always be widened to a hyperplane that still only touches it.

1The picture

On page 6 an inner product handed us the extension: project, then apply $f$. A general normed space has no perpendiculars. What it has is a unit ball, and the ball is enough. Choose a norm, a subspace $M$ and a value for $f$ on it. Then turn the candidate level line of $F$ until it stops cutting the ball.

        2Mathematical statement

        Theorem (Hahn–Banach). Let $V$ be a real vector space and $p$ a sublinear functional on it: $p(x+y)\le p(x)+p(y)$ and $p(\alpha x)=\alpha p(x)$ for $\alpha\ge0$. Let $M$ be a subspace and $f$ a linear functional on $M$ with $f(x)\le p(x)$ on $M$. Then there is a linear functional $F$ on $V$ with $F=f$ on $M$ and $F(x)\le p(x)$ on all of $V$.

        Corollary (norm-preserving extension). If $X$ is a normed space, $M$ a subspace and $f\in M'$, there is $F\in X'$ with $F=f$ on $M$ and $\|F\|=\|f\|$. Take $p(x)=\|f\|\,\|x\|$.

        The one-dimensional step. Let $x_1\notin M$. To extend $f$ to $M\oplus\mathbb Rx_1$ we need only choose the number $a=F(x_1)$. The extension satisfies $F\le p$ exactly when $$\sup_{y\in M}\bigl[-p(-y-x_1)-f(y)\bigr]\ \le\ a\ \le\ \inf_{y\in M}\bigl[p(y+x_1)-f(y)\bigr],$$ and sublinearity of $p$ guarantees that the left side does not exceed the right. The Function panel plots both brackets.

        Consequences. (a) For every $x_0\ne0$ in a normed space there is $F\in X'$ with $\|F\|=1$ and $F(x_0)=\|x_0\|$. Hence $\|x\|=\max\{|F(x)|:\|F\|=1\}$, and $X'$ separates points. (b) If $M$ is a subspace and $d=\operatorname{dist}(x_0,M)\gt0$, there is $F$ with $\|F\|=1$, $F=0$ on $M$ and $F(x_0)=d$. (c) If $A$ and $B$ are disjoint convex sets and $A$ is open, there are $F\ne0$ and $\gamma$ with $F(a)\lt\gamma\le F(b)$ for all $a\in A$, $b\in B$.

        Read (a) geometrically: through every point of the unit sphere there passes a hyperplane that does not enter the ball. At a smooth point there is one. At a corner there is a fan of them, and each gives a different norm-preserving extension. Consequence (b) is the tool that will identify annihilators in Section 3.3 and ranges of adjoints after that. It is how one proves that an operator's range is, or is not, all of the space.

        3Experiments

        Each button sets up the laboratory above and takes you back to it.

        A round ball: one tangent, one extension

        With p = 2 the candidate line cuts the ball for every position but one. The Coordinates panel shows why: the line of all extensions touches the round dual ball at a single point. That point is the Riesz vector of page 6.

        A corner: a fan of extensions

        With p = 1 and M through a corner of the diamond, the level line can swing through a right angle without entering the ball. Every position is a different extension of the same norm. In the dual picture the constraint line lies along an edge of the square.

        A flat face is not a corner

        Still p = 1, but turn M to 45°, through the middle of a face. The norm is not smooth there in any naive sense, yet only one line works: the face itself. Non-uniqueness lives at the corners of the ball, which are the flat parts of the dual ball.

        The interval for one new number

        Drag x₁ and watch the admissible interval for a = F(x₁) in the Function panel. It is never empty. At a smooth point it has length 0 wherever x₁ is. At a corner its length depends on x₁.

        Separating a disc from a square

        Turn F. The shadows F(A) and F(B) on the number line slide past each other. The directions that separate form an arc. Push the sets together and the arc shrinks to a point, then to nothing.

        Why convexity is in the hypothesis

        Put the disc in the mouth of the C-shape. The sets are disjoint, and no direction separates them: every shadow of the disc falls inside the shadow of the C.

        4What the picture proves, and what it does not

        What you may trust

        • In $\mathbb R^2$ with $M$ a line, the whole theorem is the one-dimensional step, and the screen carries it out. The admissible interval is computed from the dual ball and is never empty, in agreement with the inequality in the statement.
        • The equivalence on display is exact: $\|F\|=\|f\|$ if and only if the level set $\{F=c\}$ misses the open ball of radius $1$, if and only if the point $F$ lies in the dual ball of radius $|c|$.
        • The dual ball drawn in the Coordinates panel is the true unit ball of the dual norm, $\ell^{p'}$ with $1/p+1/p'=1$ and the inverse stretch. A later page on dual spaces computes it from scratch.
        • A failure to separate can be certified: if the shadows overlap for every one of the 720 sampled directions and the gap function is continuous with a negative maximum, no functional separates.

        What the screen cannot show

        • The theorem earns its name in infinite dimensions, where the one-dimensional step must be repeated beyond any finite count. The proof invokes Zorn's lemma, or for separable spaces a countable induction followed by extension by continuity. It asserts that $F$ exists and gives no formula for it.
        • On the screen every linear functional is continuous. In general, separation by a continuous functional needs a hypothesis such as an interior point of $A$. Two disjoint convex sets, both dense, cannot be separated by any bounded functional.
        • Uniqueness is decided by the shape of the dual ball, which you can see here but would have to prove elsewhere: if $X'$ is strictly convex, extensions are unique. In a Hilbert space that gives the projection formula of page 6.
        • Complex scalars. The theorem holds for them too (Section 6.6), but level sets of a complex functional are not hyperplanes that split the space in two, and this picture does not survive.

        5Guided questions

        1. In the one-dimensional step, prove that $-p(-y-x_1)-f(y)\le p(y'+x_1)-f(y')$ for all $y,y'\in M$. Which properties of $p$ and $f$ did you use? Show that choosing $a$ in the interval is not only sufficient but necessary for $F\le p$ on $M\oplus\mathbb Rx_1$.
        2. Let $X=\mathbb R^2$ with the $\ell^1$ norm, $M$ the horizontal axis and $f(t,0)=t$. Find all $F=(F_1,F_2)$ with $F=f$ on $M$ and $\|F\|=1$. Repeat with $M$ the diagonal and $f(t,t)=2t$. Check both answers in the laboratory. State a conjecture relating uniqueness of the extension to the shape of the dual ball at the point where the constraint line touches it, and prove it.
        3. Prove consequence (b) from the corollary: define $f$ on $M\oplus\mathbb Rx_0$ by $f(y+\alpha x_0)=\alpha d$ and show $\|f\|=1$. Deduce that a subspace $M$ is dense in $X$ if and only if the only $F\in X'$ vanishing on $M$ is $F=0$. Where on page 5 did you meet the Hilbert space version of this statement?
        4. In $\mathbb R^2$ let $A$ be the open unit disc and $B$ a closed convex set disjoint from it. Construct the separating $F$ from the point of $B$ closest to the origin, and explain where convexity of $B$ is used. Then let $A=\{(x,y):y\le0\}$ and $B=\{(x,y):x\gt0,\ xy\ge1\}$. Both are closed, convex and disjoint. Which functionals separate them? Is the separation strict? What does this example warn about?
