Every functional is a vector in disguise

A bounded linear functional on a Hilbert space slices the space into parallel level sets. One vector, perpendicular to all of them, carries everything the functional knows.

1The picture

A linear functional eats a vector and returns a number. In the Geometry panel you hold the representing vector $y$ and watch the level sets of $F(x)=\langle x,y\rangle$ follow it. In the other two panels the vectors are functions, the functional arrives as a black box, and the job is to find the $g$ hiding inside it.

2Mathematical statement

Definition. A linear functional $F$ on a normed space $X$ is bounded if $|F(x)|\le M\|x\|$ for some $M$ and all $x$. Its norm is $\|F\|=\sup_{\|x\|=1}|F(x)|$. The bounded linear functionals form the dual space $X'$.

Theorem (Riesz representation). Let $H$ be a Hilbert space. For every bounded linear functional $F$ on $H$ there is exactly one $y\in H$ with $$F(x)=\langle x,y\rangle\quad\text{for all }x\in H,\qquad\text{and}\qquad\|F\|=\|y\|.$$

The proof in four lines. Let $N=N(F)$. It is a closed subspace because $F$ is bounded. If $N=H$ take $y=0$. Otherwise choose $x_0\notin N$ and drop the perpendicular: $z=x_0-P_Nx_0\ne0$ and $z\perp N$. For any $x$ the vector $F(x)z-F(z)x$ lies in $N$, so it is orthogonal to $z$: $$F(x)\|z\|^2-F(z)\langle x,z\rangle=0,\qquad\text{that is}\qquad F(x)=\Bigl\langle x,\ \frac{F(z)}{\|z\|^2}\,z\Bigr\rangle .$$ (For complex scalars replace $F(z)$ by its conjugate.) The norm identity is the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality together with its case of equality, $x=y/\|y\|$.

The one step that is not algebra is the perpendicular. It is the closest-point construction of page 5, applied to a subspace that is closed but infinite-dimensional, and it is where completeness enters. The last two rows of the Geometry readout carry out the proof on your vectors: whatever $x$ you choose off the null space, the rebuilt vector is $y$.

The theorem says $H'$ is a copy of $H$. For other spaces the dual is a different space, and finding it is the business of Section 2.4. The point-evaluation functional in the laboratory is a first warning: it is perfectly linear, and on $L^2$ it has no representer at all.

3Experiments

Each button sets up the laboratory above and takes you back to it.

Long vector, crowded lines

Drag y outward. The level lines stay perpendicular to it and crowd together: the spacing is 1/‖y‖. Drag y around a circle and the whole family turns with it. The picture is the vector, and the vector is the picture.

Where on the unit circle is F largest?

Drag x around the unit circle and watch F(x). It peaks at ‖y‖, exactly when x points along y, and it is 0 where the circle meets the null space. That is ‖F‖ = ‖y‖.

Run the proof

Put x anywhere off the null space. The dashed perpendicular z always points along y, and the last readout rebuilds y from z and the single number F(z). Move x across the null space and see what survives.

Opening the black box

The functional “average of f over [½, ¾]” is given as a rule, not as an integral against something. Probing it with the cells ej draws its representer for you. Predict g before you look.

A functional with no representer

F(f) = f(½). On every grid there is a g, a single tall cell. Raise N: the cell gets taller, ‖F‖ = √N, and the line in the Coordinates panel climbs without end.

Two functions, drawn as two arrows

The slice view puts g along the horizontal axis and f where it truly lies relative to it. Redraw f to make F(f) = 0 and watch the arrow x land on the null space. Then press the maximizer button.

4What the picture proves, and what it does not

What you may trust

  • In $\mathbb R^n$ the theorem is the statement that a linear map to $\mathbb R$ is a row vector. Probing with a basis, $y_j=F(e_j)$, is a complete proof there, and the Coordinates panel carries it out.
  • The slice view is exact. The functions $f$ and $g$ span a plane in $L^2$, the restriction of $F$ to that plane is $\langle\cdot,g\rangle$, and the arrows have their true lengths and angle.
  • The algebra of the proof, $F(x)z-F(z)x\in N$, is checked numerically for every $x$ you drag to. It is an identity, so it cannot fail.
  • For point evaluation the computed norm is exactly $\sqrt N$. That formula, not the plot, shows the functional is unbounded for the $L^2$ norm.

What the screen cannot show

  • Boundedness is never in doubt on the screen, because every linear functional on $\mathbb R^N$ is bounded and every subspace is closed. In infinite dimensions an unbounded functional has a null space that is dense, so there is nothing to be perpendicular to, and the proof stops at its first line.
  • The perpendicular from $x_0$ to $N$ exists because $H$ is complete. In an incomplete inner product space, such as $C[0,1]$ with the $L^2$ inner product, there are bounded functionals with no representer in the space: try $F(f)=\int_0^{1/2}f$.
  • A level line for $\|F\|$ against $N$ up to 512 suggests boundedness and does not prove it. The proof is an inequality, $|F(f)|\le\|g\|\,\|f\|$.
  • The null space of $F$ in $L^2$ is a closed hyperplane: infinitely many dimensions with exactly one removed. The line and the plane on the screen get the "one removed" right and the "infinitely many" not at all.
  • The identification $H'\cong H$ is special. Already for $\ell^p$ with $p\ne2$ the representer lives in a different space, $\ell^q$.

5Guided questions

  1. Verify that $F(x)z-F(z)x\in N(F)$ for every $x$, and complete the proof of the theorem, including uniqueness of $y$. Show that a linear functional is bounded if and only if its null space is closed. Where does the proof use that $N$ is closed, and where that $H$ is complete?
  2. Prove $\|F\|=\|y\|$. In the $\mathbb R^2$ picture, the maximum of $F$ over the round unit ball is attained where a level line is tangent to the circle. Replace the circle by the $\ell^1$ diamond or the $\ell^\infty$ square of page 2. Where is the maximum now, what is $\|F\|$ in terms of $y$, and when is the maximizer not unique?
  3. Let $F(f)=f(\tfrac12)$ on continuous functions. Using a spike from page 4, show $\sup\{|F(f)|:\|f\|_2\le1\}=\infty$. Show that on the grid of $N$ cells $\|F\|=\sqrt N$ exactly. Then show that $F$ is bounded for the $H^1$ norm of page 3, so that it has a representer $g$ in $H^1$, and verify that $$g(x)=\frac{\cosh x\,\cosh(1-x_0)}{\sinh1}\ \ (x\le x_0),\qquad g(x)=\frac{\cosh x_0\,\cosh(1-x)}{\sinh1}\ \ (x\ge x_0)$$ satisfies $\int_0^1(fg+f'g')=f(x_0)$. Same functional, different Hilbert space, different vector.
  4. Show that $F\mapsto y$ is a linear, norm-preserving map of $H'$ onto $H$ for real scalars, and conjugate-linear for complex scalars. Let $K$ be the integral operator of page 1 and fix $x$. Show that $f\mapsto(Kf)(x)$ is a bounded functional on $L^2(a,b)$ and find its representer. What is the matrix of $K$ in the Coordinates panel of page 1, row by row, in these terms?
