2Mathematical statement

Definitions. For a function $f$ on $[0,1]$, $$\|f\|_{1}=\int_0^1|f|,\qquad \|f\|_{2}=\Bigl(\int_0^1|f|^2\Bigr)^{1/2},\qquad \|f\|_{\infty}=\max_{0\le x\le1}|f(x)|,$$ and, when $f$ has a derivative in the appropriate sense, $$\|f\|_{W^{1,1}}=\|f\|_1+\|f'\|_1,\qquad \|f\|_{H^1}=\bigl(\|f\|_2^2+\|f'\|_2^2\bigr)^{1/2},\qquad \|f\|_{W^{1,\infty}}=\max\bigl(\|f\|_\infty,\|f'\|_\infty\bigr).$$ The distance between $f$ and $g$ is the norm of $f-g$.

Proposition (one direction only). For every continuously differentiable $f$ on $[0,1]$, $$\|f\|_1\le\|f\|_2\le\|f\|_\infty\le\sqrt2\,\|f\|_{H^1}.$$ None of these can be reversed: there is no constant $C$ with $\|f\|_\infty\le C\|f\|_2$, none with $\|f\|_2\le C\|f\|_1$, and none with $\|f\|_{H^1}\le C\|f\|_\infty$.

Proposition (coordinates). With $\varphi_0=1$, $\varphi_k=\sqrt2\cos k\pi x$ and $c_k=\int_0^1f\varphi_k$, $$\|f\|_2^2=\sum_{k\ge0}c_k^2,\qquad \|f\|_{H^1}^2=\sum_{k\ge0}(1+k^2\pi^2)\,c_k^2 .$$ Both norms come from inner products. $L^2$ and $H^1$ are Hilbert spaces. $L^1$, $C[0,1]$ with the max norm, and the $W^{1,p}$ for $p\ne2$ are Banach spaces that are not.

On page 2 every pair of norms on $\mathbb R^n$ was equivalent, so the choice changed the shape of the ball and nothing else. Here the choice changes which sequences converge, which sets are closed, and which operators are bounded. Differentiation is unbounded from $C[0,1]$ to $C[0,1]$ but has norm at most $1$ from $H^1$ to $L^2$. Choosing the space is part of posing the problem.

3Experiments

Each button sets up the laboratory above and takes you back to it.

Close in area, far in height

A spike of height 1 and width 1/n. Raise n. The L¹ and L² distances fall, the L∞ distance stays at 1, and the H¹ distance grows. One sequence, three verdicts.

Close in height, far in slope

A wiggle sin(2πnx)/n. The two graphs merge to the eye, and every Lp distance falls. The derivative panel tells another story, and the H¹ distance never drops below about 4.4.

When all the rulers agree

Adding the constant 1/n moves f the same distance in every norm on this page. Compare the six triangles. Why is the Sobolev distance no larger than the L² distance here?

Continuous functions closing in on a step

With f the step, ramps of width 1/n approach it in L¹ and L² while staying at L∞ distance ½. The ramps form a Cauchy sequence in L² whose limit has left C[0, 1].

A right angle between functions

sin 2πx and the hat are orthogonal in L². The triangle is right-angled and Pythagoras holds exactly. Select the L∞ row: the same two functions now meet at about 97°. In which rows does the angle mean anything?

Your own pair

Draw f, switch the pencil, draw g. Try to make g close to f in L∞ and far in L¹. Then explain why you could not.

4What the picture proves, and what it does not

What you may trust

  • The triangle is honest about lengths in every row. Three numbers are the sides of a plane triangle exactly when each is at most the sum of the other two, and that is axiom (iii) of a norm.
  • For the preset families the distances have closed forms, for example $\|f-g\|_1=1/(2n)$ and $\|f-g\|_\infty=1$ for the spike. The plotted lines are those formulas, and their slopes are exact.
  • The chain $\|f\|_1\le\|f\|_2\le\|f\|_\infty$ is visible in every table you can produce and follows from the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality in two lines.
  • In the $L^2$ and $H^1$ rows the angle at $0$ is real: its cosine is $\langle f,g\rangle/\|f\|\|g\|$.

What the screen cannot show

  • In the other rows the angle is an artefact of drawing a triangle in a Euclidean plane. There is no inner product behind it, and the plane through $0$, $f$, $g$ does not look Euclidean from inside $L^1$ or $L^\infty$.
  • Each function here is 513 numbers, and on $\mathbb R^{513}$ all norms are equivalent. The step function shows a finite $H^1$ norm near 23, which is $\sqrt{1/h}$ for the grid spacing $h$. Halve the spacing and it grows by $\sqrt2$. The true value is infinite: the step is not in $H^1$.
  • A slider that stops at $n=64$ cannot show that no constant $C$ works. It shows a trend, and the proof is the closed form.
  • That the ramps have no continuous limit in $L^2$, and that there is a complete space $L^2$ in which they do converge, are statements about completion. The screen shows the Cauchy sequence, not the space that catches it.
  • A hand-drawn curve is smoothed slightly when you lift the pencil. Without that, the jitter of the hand would dominate every derivative norm, which is itself a lesson about $H^1$.

5Guided questions

  1. Prove $\|f\|_1\le\|f\|_2\le\|f\|_\infty$ on $[0,1]$. What happens to these inequalities on $[0,L]$, and on all of $\mathbb R$? For the spike of height $1$ and width $1/n$, compute the three norms exactly and deduce that neither inequality can be reversed with a constant.
  2. For $f$ continuously differentiable on $[0,1]$, start from $f(x)=f(y)+\int_y^xf'$ and integrate in $y$ to prove $\|f\|_\infty\le\|f\|_1+\|f'\|_1\le\sqrt2\,\|f\|_{H^1}$. Use the wiggle to show no inequality $\|f\|_{H^1}\le C\|f\|_\infty$ holds. Conclude that $d/dx$ is a bounded operator from $H^1$ to $L^2$ but not from $C[0,1]$ with the max norm to itself, even on the subspace where it is defined.
  3. Let $g_n$ be the ramp of width $1/n$. Compute $\|g_n-g_m\|_2$ and show $(g_n)$ is Cauchy in the $L^2$ norm. Show that if $g_n\to g$ in $L^2$ with $g$ continuous, then $g=0$ on $[0,\tfrac12)$ and $g=1$ on $(\tfrac12,1]$, which is impossible. What does this say about $C[0,1]$ with the $L^2$ norm, and why did page 1 use the max norm?
  4. Verify that the $\varphi_k$ are orthonormal, and for $f\in C^1[0,1]$ integrate by parts to show that the sine coefficients of $f'$ are $-k\pi c_k$. Deduce the formula for $\|f\|_{H^1}^2$. Which sequences $(c_k)$ are the coordinates of a function in $H^1$? Show that the unit ball of $H^1$, viewed inside $L^2$, is an ellipsoid whose $k$-th semi-axis is $(1+k^2\pi^2)^{-1/2}$. Keep this picture. It is what a compact operator does to a ball.
