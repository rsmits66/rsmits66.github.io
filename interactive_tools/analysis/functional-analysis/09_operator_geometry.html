What an operator does to a ball

A linear operator is known by the body it makes out of the unit ball. How far that body reaches is the norm. How close it comes to the origin decides whether the operator can be undone.

1The picture

Chapter 2 studied maps from a normed space to the scalars. Now the target is another normed space. Set the four entries of $A$, choose a norm on each side, and watch the unit ball of $X$ turn into a body in $Y$. Two numbers summarize it: the largest stretch $\|A\|$ and the smallest stretch $m(A)$.

2Mathematical statement

Definitions. A linear operator $A:X\to Y$ between normed spaces is bounded if $\|Ax\|\le M\|x\|$ for some $M$ and all $x$. Its norm and its minimum modulus are $$\|A\|=\sup_{x\ne0}\frac{\|Ax\|}{\|x\|},\qquad m(A)=\inf_{x\ne0}\frac{\|Ax\|}{\|x\|}.$$ The null space is $N(A)=\{x:Ax=0\}$ and the range is $R(A)=\{Ax:x\in X\}$. The set $B(X,Y)$ of bounded operators is a normed space, and a Banach space whenever $Y$ is.

Theorem. For a linear operator $A$ the following are equivalent: $A$ is continuous; $A$ is continuous at $0$; $A$ is bounded; $A$ maps the unit ball into some ball.

Theorem (bounded below). $m(A)\gt0$ if and only if $A$ is one-to-one and $A^{-1}:R(A)\to X$ is bounded, and then $\|A^{-1}\|=1/m(A)$. If $X$ is a Banach space and $m(A)\gt0$, then $R(A)$ is closed.

Formulas in $\mathbb R^n$. From $\ell^1$ to $\ell^1$, $\|A\|$ is the largest absolute column sum. From $\ell^\infty$ to $\ell^\infty$ it is the largest absolute row sum. From $\ell^2$ to $\ell^2$, $\|A\|=\sigma_1$ and $m(A)=\sigma_n$, the largest and smallest singular values, and the image of the ball is an ellipsoid with semi-axes $\sigma_iu_i=Av_i$.

In the picture, $\|A\|$ is the radius of the smallest ball of $Y$ that contains the image, and $m(A)$ is the radius of the largest ball that the image of the sphere stays outside of. When $A$ is onto, that inner ball lies inside the image of the ball, which is the quantitative content of the open mapping theorem of Section 3.7. The two numbers $\|A\|$ and $m(A)$ will return in every chapter: perturbing $A$ by an operator of norm less than $m(A)$ cannot create a null space, and that is the first stability theorem on the way to Fredholm operators.

3Experiments

Each button sets up the laboratory above and takes you back to it.

An ellipse and its axes

With Euclidean norms on both sides the image is an ellipse. Its long semi-axis has length ‖A‖ and its short one m(A). Slide x to the crimson direction, then the gray one, and watch Ax reach the two dashed circles.

The norm is not the largest eigenvalue

The shear has both eigenvalues equal to 1, and it still stretches some vectors by 1.62. It also shrinks others to 0.62. Eigenvalues describe directions that are kept. The norm asks about all directions.

A rigid motion that is not rigid

Rotation by 45° has ‖A‖ = m(A) = 1 for the Euclidean norm. Change both exponents to 1. The diamond turns into a square that pokes out of the diamond of Y, and ‖A‖ = √2. Whether an operator is an isometry depends on the norms.

Collapse

Move a₂₂ from 1.1 down to 1. The image flattens, the inner ball shrinks to a point, and at 1 the image is a segment inside the line R(A) while a whole line N(A) is sent to 0. Just before that, the inverse has norm 1/m(A), as large as you please.

Read the norm off the matrix

With p = r = 1 the most stretched direction is always a coordinate axis, and ‖A‖ is the larger column sum. Switch both to ∞: now it is a corner of the square and the larger row sum. Check against the entries.

No kernel, and still not bounded below

In the lower Function plot choose integration. No ek is sent to 0, but the stretch 1/kπ sinks toward 0. Then choose differentiation, where it climbs without limit. Neither can happen to a matrix.

4What the picture proves, and what it does not

What you may trust

  • For $p=r=2$ the numbers come from the singular value decomposition and are exact. For $p,r\in\{1,\infty\}$ the maximum is attained at a corner of the ball and is also exact. For other exponents the sphere is searched at 1440 points and refined, which is good to the digits shown.
  • The sandwich is a theorem: the image of the unit sphere lies between the spheres of radius $m(A)$ and $\|A\|$ in $Y$, and touches both.
  • For a square matrix, $m(A)\gt0$, $N(A)=\{0\}$, $R(A)=Y$ and $\det A\ne0$ all say the same thing. The laboratory shows them failing together.
  • In the lower plot the stretches of integration and differentiation on $e_k$ are exactly $\sqrt3/(k\pi)$ and $k\pi$. The trends are formulas, not measurements.

What the screen cannot show

  • Every matrix is bounded, and its supremum is attained because the sphere is compact. In infinite dimensions an operator can be unbounded, and a bounded one need not attain its norm.
  • For matrices, one-to-one and bounded below are the same, and both are the same as onto. In infinite dimensions all three separate. Integration is one-to-one with $m=0$. The right shift on $\ell^2$ has $m=1$ and is not onto. Sorting out which combinations can occur, and how stable they are, is Fredholm theory.
  • The image of the closed unit ball is closed and bounded here. In general it need not be closed, and whether its closure is compact is the definition of a compact operator in Chapter 4.
  • The lower plot tests an operator on one sequence of vectors. That gives a lower bound for $\|T\|$ and an upper bound for $m(T)$ and nothing more. For multiplication by $1+x$ the true norm is $2$, and no sine reaches it.

5Guided questions

  1. Prove that from $\ell^1_n$ to $\ell^1_n$ the norm of $A$ is the largest absolute column sum. (A convex function on the diamond is largest at a corner.) Use page 8 to deduce the row-sum formula for $\ell^\infty_n$ from it. What is $\|A\|$ from $\ell^1_n$ to $\ell^\infty_n$? Check all three in the laboratory.
  2. For Euclidean norms show $\|A\|^2$ is the largest eigenvalue of $A^{\mathsf T}A$ and $m(A)^2$ the smallest. For the shear $\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$ compute both and their product. Why must the product be $|\det A|$? Give a sequence of matrices with all eigenvalues equal to $1$ and $\|A_n\|\to\infty$.
  3. Prove the theorem on operators bounded below, including the closed range. Then let $T$ be integration on $L^2(0,1)$, $Tf(x)=\int_0^xf$. Show $N(T)=\{0\}$, $\|Te_k\|\to0$, and conclude $m(T)=0$. Describe $R(T)$ and show it is dense but not all of $L^2$. Hence $R(T)$ is not closed: no matrix behaves this way.
  4. Let $A,B\in B(X,Y)$ with $\|B\|\lt m(A)$. Show that $m(A+B)\ge m(A)-\|B\|\gt0$, so $A+B$ is still one-to-one with closed range. In the laboratory, take the nearly singular matrix and find the smallest change of one entry that creates a kernel. Compare its size with $m(A)$ in the norm you chose. Can a perturbation smaller than $m(A)$ ever create a kernel?
