Every ball has a polar twin

The size of a functional is the most it can extract from the unit ball. Measure every functional that way and their unit ball appears: corners where the first ball had faces, faces where it had corners, and ℓq where it was ℓp.

2Mathematical statement

Definitions. For a bounded linear functional $F$ on a normed space $X$, $\ \|F\|=\sup\{F(x):\|x\|\le1\}$. The polar of a set $B\subset X$ is $B^\circ=\{F\in X':F(x)\le1\text{ for all }x\in B\}$. The unit ball of $X'$ is the polar of the unit ball of $X$.

Theorem (Hölder, and its case of equality). Let $1\le p\le\infty$ and $\tfrac1p+\tfrac1q=1$. For $x,F\in\mathbb R^n$, $$\Bigl|\sum_iF_ix_i\Bigr|\le\|F\|_q\,\|x\|_p ,$$ and for every $F$ there is $x^*$ with $\|x^*\|_p=1$ and $F(x^*)=\|F\|_q$. For $1\lt p\lt\infty$ it is unique: $x^*_i=\operatorname{sign}(F_i)\,|F_i|^{q-1}/\|F\|_q^{\,q-1}$. Consequently the dual of $\ell^p_n$ is $\ell^q_n$, with equal norms.

Theorem (dual spaces of sequences). For $1\le p\lt\infty$, every bounded linear functional on $\ell^p$ has the form $F(x)=\sum_iF_ix_i$ with $(F_i)\in\ell^q$ and $\|F\|=\|(F_i)\|_q$. So $(\ell^p)'=\ell^q$; in particular $(\ell^1)'=\ell^\infty$. Also $(c_0)'=\ell^1$. But $(\ell^\infty)'$ is strictly larger than $\ell^1$.

Change of variables. If $\|x\|=\|Ax\|_p$ with $A$ invertible, then $\|F\|=\|A^{-\mathsf T}F\|_q$. Stretching the ball in one direction squeezes the polar ball in that direction.

The argument for $(\ell^p)'=\ell^q$ is the Coordinates panel of page 6 over again: probe $F$ with the unit vectors to find the numbers $F_i=F(e_i)$, then feed $F$ the maximizer of the theorem, truncated to $n$ terms, to show that $\sum_{i\le n}|F_i|^q\le\|F\|^q$ for every $n$. It breaks down at $p=\infty$ because the truncations of a bounded sequence need not converge to it in the $\ell^\infty$ norm. The functionals that escape are produced by the Hahn–Banach theorem, and none of them can be written down.

3Experiments

Each button sets up the laboratory above and takes you back to it.

The ball that is its own twin

At p = 2 the two balls coincide, the maximizer points along F, and the bars of x* are the bars of F rescaled. This is page 6. Every other p breaks the symmetry.

Diamond and square

With p = 1, drag F slowly around. The maximizer sits at one corner of the diamond, then jumps to the next. At the instant of the jump F/‖F‖ is at a corner of the square, and a whole edge of the diamond maximizes.

Winner takes all, or everyone votes

In ℝ⁸, run p from 1 to ∞. At p = 1 the maximizer spends its whole budget on the largest bar of F. At p = ∞ every coordinate contributes ±1 regardless of size. In between, x* is F raised to the power q − 1.

Conjugate exponents

p = 4/3 pairs with q = 4. Overlay the other ball: one bulges where the other is pinched, and they cross on the diagonals. Find the p whose ball touches its twin along the axes and along the diagonals.

Stretch one, squeeze the other

Stretch and tilt the ball of X. The polar ball tilts the same way and is squeezed along the long axis. A functional has less room to be large where the ball reaches further.

Corners become faces

Drag the three free corners of the hexagon. Each corner v of the ball is a face { F : F(v) = 1 } of the polar ball, and each face of the ball is a corner of the polar. Count both. Make one corner redundant and watch a face of the polar disappear.

4What the picture proves, and what it does not

What you may trust

  • The polar ball on the screen is computed from the formula $\|A^{-\mathsf T}F\|_q$, and the Function panel checks it against a direct search over the sphere. The two agree, which is Hölder's inequality with its case of equality, in $\mathbb R^2$.
  • The correspondence between corners and faces is exact for polygons: $\|F\|=\max_i|F(v_i)|$ over the corners $v_i$, so the polar ball is cut out by one pair of lines per pair of corners.
  • Taking the polar twice returns the original ball. On the screen you can see it: the gray line $\{G:G(x)=1\}$ supports the polar ball for every $x$ on the sphere. This is consequence (a) of page 7, that $\|x\|=\max\{F(x):\|F\|\le1\}$.

What the screen cannot show

  • Two planes that look alike are being kept apart on purpose. $X$ and $X'$ are different spaces, and drawing $F$ as an arrow in the plane of $X$ uses the dot product, which is a convention and not part of the structure. Only at $p=2$ is there a natural identification.
  • In $\mathbb R^n$ the maximum is attained because the ball is compact. In infinite dimensions the supremum defining $\|F\|$ need not be attained. On $c_0$ the functional $F=(2^{-i})$ has $\|F\|=1$ and no maximizer.
  • In $\mathbb R^n$ the double dual is the space itself. For sequence spaces, $(c_0)''=\ell^\infty\ne c_0$ and $(\ell^1)''\supsetneq\ell^1$. Spaces that do equal their double dual are called reflexive, and they are the subject of Chapter 8.
  • At $p=\infty$ the ℝ⁸ bars look as harmless as at any other $p$. The dual of $\ell^\infty$ contains functionals, such as generalized limits, that vanish on every finitely supported sequence. No finite number of bars can display one.

5Guided questions

  1. Verify that the $x^*$ of the theorem satisfies $\|x^*\|_p=1$ and $F(x^*)=\|F\|_q$. Describe all maximizers when $p=1$ and when $p=\infty$, and say exactly when the maximizer is unique. Compare with what the laboratory reports in its "maximizers" row.
  2. Prove the change-of-variables rule $\|F\|=\|A^{-\mathsf T}F\|_q$. For $A=\operatorname{diag}(a,1/a)$ and $p=2$, both balls are ellipses. Find their semi-axes and check that the product of the two areas does not depend on $a$. Does it depend on $p$? Compute it for $p=1$ and $p=2$.
  3. Let $F$ be a bounded linear functional on $\ell^p$, $1\le p\lt\infty$, and put $F_i=F(e_i)$. Using the maximizer of the theorem for $(F_1,\dots,F_n,0,0,\dots)$, show $\sum_{i\le n}|F_i|^q\le\|F\|^q$. Then show $F(x)=\sum F_ix_i$ for all $x\in\ell^p$. Where does the last step use $p\lt\infty$? Which subspace of $\ell^\infty$ would the same argument handle?
  4. Let $B$ be the hexagon with corners $\pm v_1,\pm v_2,\pm v_3$. Show that $B^\circ=\{F:|F(v_i)|\le1,\ i=1,2,3\}$ and that $(B^\circ)^\circ=B$. Now let $B$ be any closed, convex, symmetric set with $0$ in its interior. Use the separation theorem of page 7 to prove $(B^\circ)^\circ=B$. What goes wrong if $B$ is not convex? Try the star-shaped balls of page 2 with $p\lt1$: what is their polar?
