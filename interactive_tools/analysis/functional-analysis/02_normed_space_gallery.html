2Mathematical statement

Definition. A norm on a vector space $X$ assigns to each $x$ a real number $\|x\|$ such that

(i) $\|x\|\ge0$, and $\|x\|=0$ only for $x=0$;   (ii) $\|\alpha x\|=|\alpha|\,\|x\|$ for every scalar $\alpha$;   (iii) $\|x+y\|\le\|x\|+\|y\|$.

The family on this page. Fix weights $w_i\gt0$ and an invertible matrix $A$ (here a rotation by $\theta$ followed by the stretch $\operatorname{diag}(a,1/a,1)$). For $z=Ax$ put $$\|x\|=\Bigl(\sum_i w_i|z_i|^p\Bigr)^{1/p}\quad(0\lt p\lt\infty),\qquad \|x\|=\max_i|z_i|\quad(p=\infty).$$

Theorem (Minkowski). For $1\le p\le\infty$ this is a norm, and $\mathbb R^n$ with it is a Banach space. For $0\lt p\lt1$ axioms (i) and (ii) hold and (iii) fails; what remains true is $\|x+y\|^p\le\|x\|^p+\|y\|^p$.

Proposition (the ball knows). Suppose $N$ satisfies (i) and (ii). Then $N$ satisfies (iii) if and only if the set $\{x:N(x)\le1\}$ is convex.

Proposition (the limit). For plain $\ell^p$ on $\mathbb R^n$, $\ \|x\|_\infty\le\|x\|_p\le n^{1/p}\|x\|_\infty$. Hence $\|x\|_p\to\|x\|_\infty$ as $p\to\infty$, and the same limit holds with any weights.

Two different things can bend a ball. A weight changes how much each coordinate counts inside the sum. It is a change of measure, and it fades away as $p\to\infty$ because a maximum does not care how wide a step is. A stretch or tilt is a linear change of variable. It persists for every $p$, and at $p=2$ it produces exactly the ellipses, the unit balls of inner products.

The sequence spaces $\ell^p$ and the function spaces $L^p$ of the coming sections are these same formulas with infinitely many coordinates. The Function panel is the bridge: a weighted $\ell^p$ norm on $\mathbb R^n$ is the $L^p$ norm of a step function.

3Experiments

Each button sets up the laboratory above and takes you back to it.

Diamond, disc, square

Sweep p upward. The ball grows from a four-pointed star through the diamond at p = 1 and the disc at p = 2 toward the square. Notice the single moment at which the sides stop caving in.

Breaking the triangle inequality

With p = ½, x = (1, 0) and y = (0, 1) have length 1 each, yet x + y has length 4. The chord joining them leaves the ball, which is the same failure seen as a shape.

A pair that hides the failure

Still p = ½, but now x and y point the same way. For this pair (iii) holds. One passing pair proves nothing. One failing pair settles the matter.

Weights fade, stretches stay

Start with weights 2 and ½ at p = 1, a lopsided diamond. Sweep p to ∞ and the ball becomes the plain square. Then try the same with a stretch instead of a weight.

The only balls that are ellipses

At p = 2 with a stretch and a tilt, the last readout, the parallelogram defect, is 0 for every x and y. Move p away from 2 and it is not. That number detects inner products.

Slicing the octahedron

In ℝ³ at p = 1, move the slice height c. Each slice is a smaller diamond. Repeat at p = ∞, where every slice is the same square, and at p = 2.

4What the picture proves, and what it does not

What you may trust

  • A failure is a proof. The pair $x=(1,0)$, $y=(0,1)$ at $p=\tfrac12$ gives $\|x+y\|=4\gt2$. One counterexample shows (iii) is false, and the screen computes it exactly.
  • The link between the chord and the inequality is a theorem, not an impression: by homogeneity, (iii) for $x,y$ is the statement that a certain point of the chord from $x/\|x\|$ to $y/\|y\|$ lies in the ball.
  • At $p=2$ the parallelogram defect vanishes identically. You can check this by algebra in two lines, since $\|x\|^2=x^{\mathsf T}(A^{\mathsf T}WA)\,x$.
  • In $\mathbb R^n$ any two of these balls fit inside multiples of each other. So all these norms give the same convergent sequences, and completeness is inherited from $\mathbb R$.

What the screen cannot show

  • A success is not a proof. The ball looks convex for every $p\ge1$, but seeing no dent among a few hundred sampled points is not Minkowski's inequality. The proof goes through Hölder's inequality.
  • Everything special about finite dimensions is invisible here because it is everywhere. The constant $n^{1/p}$ that compares $\|\cdot\|_p$ with $\|\cdot\|_\infty$ grows without bound with $n$. In the sequence spaces the norms are no longer equivalent: $\ell^1\subsetneq\ell^2\subsetneq\ell^\infty$, and a sequence can converge in one norm and not in another.
  • These unit balls are compact. In an infinite-dimensional normed space the closed unit ball never is. No picture in $\mathbb R^3$ can hint at that, and much of the theory of compact and Fredholm operators exists to cope with it.
  • Completeness costs nothing in $\mathbb R^n$. For $C[a,b]$ with an integral norm it fails, which is why the choice of norm on page 1 mattered.
  • The $\mathbb R^3$ surface is a mesh of about a thousand flat pieces. Edges and corners of the cube and the octahedron are slightly rounded by it, and the spikes for $p\lt1$ are blunter than the truth.

5Guided questions

  1. Let $N$ satisfy (i) and (ii). For nonzero $x,y$ put $t=N(y)/(N(x)+N(y))$ and show that $\dfrac{x+y}{N(x)+N(y)}=(1-t)\dfrac{x}{N(x)}+t\,\dfrac{y}{N(y)}$. Deduce the proposition: (iii) holds if and only if the unit ball is convex. In the laboratory the readout uses the midpoint of the chord. Find a pair for which the midpoint is inside the ball although (iii) fails for that pair, and explain why this does not contradict the proposition.
  2. For plain $\ell^p$ compute $\|e_1+e_2\|_p$ and find exactly the $p$ for which (iii) fails for this pair. Then prove $(s+t)^p\le s^p+t^p$ for $s,t\ge0$ and $0\lt p\lt1$, and conclude that $d(x,y)=\sum_i|x_i-y_i|^p$ is a metric on $\mathbb R^n$. Is its unit ball convex? Is $d(\alpha x,0)=|\alpha|\,d(x,0)$?
  3. Prove $\|x\|_\infty\le\|x\|_p\le n^{1/p}\|x\|_\infty$ and the limit $p\to\infty$. Extend the argument to positive weights and explain the experiment "Weights fade, stretches stay". Then let $n\to\infty$: exhibit a sequence in $\ell^2$ that is not in $\ell^1$, and a sequence of vectors with $\|x^{(k)}\|_\infty\to0$ but $\|x^{(k)}\|_1=1$.
  4. Show that at $p=2$ the norm on this page comes from the inner product $\langle x,y\rangle=x^{\mathsf T}Gy$ with $G=A^{\mathsf T}WA$, and verify the parallelogram law. For plain $\ell^p$ on $\mathbb R^2$, evaluate the parallelogram defect at $x=e_1$, $y=e_2$ and show it vanishes only for $p=2$. Which shapes of unit ball, then, can belong to an inner product?
