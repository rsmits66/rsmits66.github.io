Five ways to converge

A sequence of functions can settle down at each point, in a band, in area, in energy, or only in its shadows. Seven standard sequences, five verdicts each, and no two sequences alike.

2Mathematical statement

Definitions. Let $f_n,f$ be functions on $[0,1]$.

Pointwise: $f_n(x)\to f(x)$ for each $x$.   Uniform: $\|f_n-f\|_\infty\to0$.   In $L^1$: $\int|f_n-f|\to0$.   In $L^2$: $\int|f_n-f|^2\to0$.

Weakly in $L^2$: $\ \langle f_n,g\rangle=\int f_ng\to\int fg\ $ for every $g\in L^2$. In a general normed space $X$, $x_n\to x$ weakly means $x'(x_n)\to x'(x)$ for every bounded linear functional $x'$ on $X$.

Proposition (what implies what). On $[0,1]$, $$\text{uniform}\ \Longrightarrow\ L^2\ \Longrightarrow\ L^1,\qquad \text{uniform}\ \Longrightarrow\ \text{pointwise},\qquad L^2\ \Longrightarrow\ \text{weak in }L^2 .$$ No other implication among the five holds in general. The seven sequences in the laboratory are the counterexamples.

Theorem (weak convergence in coordinates). Let $(e_k)$ be an orthonormal basis of a Hilbert space. Then $f_n\to f$ weakly if and only if $\sup_n\|f_n\|\lt\infty$ and $\langle f_n,e_k\rangle\to\langle f,e_k\rangle$ for each $k$. Moreover a weakly convergent sequence satisfies $\|f\|\le\liminf\|f_n\|$, and if in addition $\|f_n\|\to\|f\|$ then $f_n\to f$ in norm.

The boundedness in the theorem is not a convenience. It is forced by the uniform boundedness principle of Section 3.6, and the tall spike shows what goes wrong without it. Weak convergence is the subject of Section 8.4, where it supplies the compactness that the unit ball of an infinite-dimensional space lacks: bounded sequences in a reflexive space have weakly convergent subsequences. A compact operator is precisely one that turns weak convergence into norm convergence, which is why this page comes before Chapter 4.

3Experiments

Each button sets up the laboratory above and takes you back to it.

Pointwise without uniform

For xⁿ, put x₀ at 0.8, then 0.95, then 0.99. Each time the dots fall to 0, later and later. However large n is, the graph still climbs out of the band near x = 1.

Converging in area, nowhere at a point

The typewriter block gets narrower, so its area falls to 0. But it keeps sweeping across, and every x₀ is hit again and again. Watch the dots: infinitely many 1s, infinitely many 0s.

Only the shadows converge

sin 2πnx keeps L² distance 1/√2 from 0 forever. Yet against any g you choose, the shaded area cancels. In the Coordinates panel the bars slide to the right and off the edge.

Same shape, three fates

A block of width 1/n with height 1, √n, or n. Compare “Spike of height √n” with “Spike of height n”. One converges weakly to 0. The other has shadows that converge, to g(0), but there is no function f with ⟨f, g⟩ = g(0).

The overshoot that never leaves

Fourier sums of a block converge in L², in L¹, and at every point. Yet no n fits a band with ε below ½, because each sum is continuous and must pass through the jump. Look beyond the corner too: the overshoot of about 9 percent of the jump gets narrower, never lower.

A shadow that fools you

With g = 1, the shadow of sin 2πnx is exactly 0 for every n, and the shadow of the spike of height n is exactly 1. One test function proves nothing. Draw your own g and try to keep the oscillation's shadow from shrinking.

4What the picture proves, and what it does not

What you may trust

  • For the first five sequences the three distances are closed forms, for example $\|x^n\|_1=1/(n+1)$ and $\|x^n\|_\infty=1$. The plotted lines are those formulas, not measurements.
  • A single $g$ whose shadow fails to converge proves that weak convergence fails. So does an unbounded norm. These negative verdicts can be certified from the screen.
  • For the oscillation the coordinates are computable by hand, and each one tends to $0$ while $\sum_kc_k^2=\tfrac12$ for every $n$. The picture of energy leaving through the right edge is accurate.

What the screen cannot show

  • Convergence is a statement about all large $n$, and the slider stops at 128. A sequence that looks settled may not be, and the typewriter's failure at every point is visible only as a pattern you must extrapolate.
  • Weak convergence quantifies over every $g$ in $L^2$. The screen tests one $g$ at a time. The positive verdict for the oscillation is the Riemann–Lebesgue lemma, not an observation.
  • The point $x=1$, where $x^n\to1$, is not a pixel. Pointwise statements about single points and statements "almost everywhere" are invisible on any grid.
  • The spike of height $n$ is drawn clipped. Its shadows converge to $g(0)$ for continuous $g$, which describes a point mass at $0$. That object lives in the dual of $C[0,1]$ (Section 2.4), not in $L^2$.
  • Beyond $n=128$ the grid of 2048 points cannot resolve $\sin2\pi nx$. Any computed picture of weak convergence eventually turns into aliasing.

5Guided questions

  1. Prove the three implications of the proposition. For each of the following, name the sequence from the laboratory that shows the implication fails: pointwise $\Rightarrow L^1$; $L^1\Rightarrow$ pointwise; $L^1\Rightarrow L^2$; weak $\Rightarrow L^2$; pointwise $\Rightarrow$ weak.
  2. For $f_n=x^n$ and a given $\varepsilon\lt1$, find the set of $x$ where $f_n(x)\gt\varepsilon$ and its length. Use this to explain, in one sentence each, why the convergence is not uniform on $[0,1]$, is uniform on $[0,b]$ for every $b\lt1$, and does hold in $L^1$.
  3. Compute $c_k=\int_0^1\sin(2\pi nx)\,\sqrt2\cos(k\pi x)\,dx$. Show $c_k=0$ for even $k$, and that for fixed $k$, $c_k\to0$ as $n\to\infty$. Near which $k$ are the bars largest? Deduce from the theorem that $\sin2\pi nx\to0$ weakly, and check $\|0\|\le\liminf\|f_n\|$. Why does the last part of the theorem not apply?
  4. Let $f_n=n^{\alpha}\chi_{(0,1/n)}$. For which $\alpha$ does $f_n\to0$ pointwise, in $L^1$, in $L^2$, weakly in $L^2$? For $\alpha=1$ show $\int f_ng\to g(0)$ for continuous $g$, and show there is no $f\in L^2$ with $\int fg=g(0)$ for all continuous $g$. Now let $K$ be the integral operator of page 1. Show $Kf_n\to0$ uniformly when $\alpha=\tfrac12$. An operator that improves weak convergence to norm convergence is called compact.
