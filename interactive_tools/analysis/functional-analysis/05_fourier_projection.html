Pick any finite set of sines and cosines. The Fourier coefficients give the closest point of their span, the error stands perpendicular to everything you picked, and the energy splits exactly in two.

2Mathematical statement

Theorem (projection onto a finite orthonormal set). Let $e_1,\dots,e_m$ be orthonormal in a space with inner product $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$ and norm $\|x\|=\langle x,x\rangle^{1/2}$, let $V$ be their span, and put $Pf=\sum_{j}\langle f,e_j\rangle e_j$. Then

(a)   $\langle f-Pf,\,e_j\rangle=0$ for each $j$, so $f-Pf\perp V$;

(b)   for every $v\in V$, $\ \|f-v\|^2=\|f-Pf\|^2+\|Pf-v\|^2$, so $Pf$ is the unique closest point of $V$ to $f$;

(c)   $\|f\|^2=\sum_j|\langle f,e_j\rangle|^2+\|f-Pf\|^2$. In particular $\sum_j|\langle f,e_j\rangle|^2\le\|f\|^2$ (Bessel's inequality).

Theorem (Parseval). For an orthonormal sequence $(e_j)$ in a Hilbert space the following are equivalent: the finite sums $\sum_{j\le m}\langle f,e_j\rangle e_j$ converge to $f$ for every $f$; $\ \|f\|^2=\sum_j|\langle f,e_j\rangle|^2$ for every $f$; the only $f$ orthogonal to every $e_j$ is $0$. The trigonometric system in $L^2(0,1)$ has these properties.

Nothing in the first theorem mentions sines. It is a statement about inner products, and the proof of (a) is one line: $\langle f-Pf,e_j\rangle=\langle f,e_j\rangle-\langle f,e_j\rangle$. Parts (b) and (c) are Pythagoras applied to the right angle that (a) provides. The map $P$ is linear, satisfies $P^2=P$ and $\|Pf\|\le\|f\|$, and will return as the model of a bounded operator, of a projection in Chapter 6, and of a selfadjoint operator in Chapter 13.

The closest-point property is also the engine of the next chapter. The Riesz representation theorem of Section 2.1 is proved by dropping a perpendicular onto the null space of a functional, and that needs a closest point in a subspace that is closed but no longer finite-dimensional. Completeness is what rescues the argument there.

3Experiments

Each button sets up the laboratory above and takes you back to it.

One right angle at a time

Start with V empty, so r = f. Step the inspector through the columns and add them one by one. Each addition flattens one bar of the bottom row and makes the shaded area under r · ej cancel exactly.

A subspace that sees nothing

The block is symmetric about x = ½ and every sine is antisymmetric. Project onto sixteen sines and get Pf = 0. A large subspace is no help if f is perpendicular to it.

Try to beat the Fourier coefficient

Detune one coefficient by t. The competitor v stays in V, and ‖f − v‖² rises by exactly t², whichever vector you detune and whatever f is. In the Geometry panel the triangle f, Pf, v keeps its right angle.

An error of exactly zero

f = sin 2πx + ½ cos 6πx lies in a two-dimensional subspace. Find it. With the right two columns the residual vanishes identically, and twenty wrong columns leave it untouched.

Which six would you pick?

For the sawtooth, compare the first six vectors with the six largest bars. The error plot shows both strategies for every m. For which of the preset functions do the two curves coincide, and why?

Smooth against rough

Compare the error curve of the smooth wave with that of the block. One falls off a cliff and the other crawls along a line of slope −½. The energy bar's pale segment tells the same story.

4What the picture proves, and what it does not

What you may trust

  • The Geometry panel is not an analogy. The points $0$, $f$, $Pf$ and $v$ lie in the span of three orthonormal functions, and the picture is that span drawn to scale. Lengths and angles are the true $L^2$ lengths and angles.
  • On a uniform grid of 1024 points the sampled sines and cosines up to frequency 16 are exactly orthogonal as vectors of numbers. So the zeros in the bottom row are exact up to rounding, about $10^{-17}$.
  • Parts (a), (b), (c) of the theorem are finite algebra. Every instance you can produce on the screen is an instance of a proved identity, and the identities $\|f\|^2=\|Pf\|^2+\|r\|^2$ and $\|f-v\|^2=\|r\|^2+t^2$ hold to every displayed digit.

What the screen cannot show

  • Completeness. The pale segment of the energy bar is the energy beyond the 33 columns. That it shrinks to zero as columns are added without end, for every $f\in L^2$, is Parseval's theorem for the trigonometric system. It needs an approximation argument, and no finite table can supply it.
  • Closest points in infinite-dimensional subspaces. Here $V$ is finite-dimensional, and the formula for $Pf$ is a finite sum. For a closed subspace in general, the closest point exists because a Hilbert space is complete. In an incomplete inner product space it can fail to exist.
  • Anything about pointwise convergence. The error here is an $L^2$ error. For the block it tends to $0$ while the overshoot at the jumps never does, as page 4 showed.
  • Spaces without an inner product. In $L^1$ or $C[0,1]$ there is no right angle, the closest point of a subspace need not be unique, and the Fourier sum is not the best approximation. The ease of this page is a property of $L^2$.

5Guided questions

  1. Prove (a), then expand $\|f-v\|^2=\|(f-Pf)+(Pf-v)\|^2$ to prove (b), and deduce (c). Where exactly did you use that the $e_j$ are orthonormal and not merely linearly independent? If they were only independent, which statement would survive, with $Pf$ defined as the closest point?
  2. Show that $P$ is linear, $P^2=P$, $\langle Pf,g\rangle=\langle f,Pg\rangle$, and $\|Pf\|\le\|f\|$ with equality exactly when $f\in V$. In the laboratory, what do these four facts look like? Conversely, let $Q$ be linear with $Q^2=Q$ and $\|Qf\|\le\|f\|$ for all $f$. Show that $f-Qf\perp Qg$ for all $f,g$. (For $Qf=0$ and $g=Qg$, compare $\|g\|$ with $\|g+sf\|$ for all real $s$.)
  3. Compute the coefficients of the block $\chi_{(1/4,3/4)}$. Show that with the first $m$ vectors the squared error is a tail $\sum_{k\gt K}\tfrac{2}{\pi^2k^2}$ over odd $k$ and behaves like $c/m$. Explain the slope $-\tfrac12$ in the error plot. What decay of coefficients, and what slope, do you expect for the hat? Check it.
  4. Let $W$ be the set of $f\in L^2(0,1)$ with $\int_0^1f=0$. Show $W$ is a closed subspace, find the closest point of $W$ to a given $f$, and identify the residual. Now let $F(f)=\int_0^1f(x)g(x)\,dx$ for a fixed $g\ne0$ and let $W=N(F)$. Show that the residual of any $f$ is a multiple of $g$. Turn this around: given only a bounded linear functional $F$ on a Hilbert space, how would you find the $g$ that represents it?
