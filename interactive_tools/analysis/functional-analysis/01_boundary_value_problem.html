A problem from differential equations

A differential equation becomes an integral equation, the integral equation becomes f = u + Kf, and the course begins: when can I − K be inverted?

2Mathematical statement

The solved problem. Let $g\in C[a,b]$. A function $f$ satisfies $$f''+f=g\ \text{ on }[a,b],\qquad f(a)=1,\quad f'(a)=0 \tag{1.1, 1.2}$$ if and only if $$f(x)=\cos(x-a)+\int_a^x\sin(x-t)\,g(t)\,dt .$$

The generalization. Let $\sigma\in C[a,b]$. Putting $g=\sigma f$ in the formula, $f$ solves $f''+f=\sigma f$ with the same initial data if and only if $$f(x)=\cos(x-a)+\int_a^x\sin(x-t)\,\sigma(t)\,f(t)\,dt. \tag{1.7}$$ With the shorthand $u(x)=\cos(x-a)$ and $Kh(x)=\int_a^x\sin(x-t)\sigma(t)h(t)\,dt$ this reads $$f=u+Kf,\qquad\text{that is,}\qquad (I-K)f=u. \tag{1.9}$$

Theorem. Give $C[a,b]$ the norm $\|h\|=\max_{a\le x\le b}|h(x)|$ and let $M=\|\sigma\|$. Then for every $h\in C[a,b]$ and every $n$, $$|K^nh(x)|\le\frac{M^n(x-a)^n}{n!}\,\|h\|,\qquad\text{so}\qquad \|K^nh\|\le\frac{\bigl(M(b-a)\bigr)^n}{n!}\,\|h\| .$$ Consequently the partial sums $f_n=u+Ku+\dots+K^nu$ form a Cauchy sequence in $C[a,b]$. Because $C[a,b]$ is complete they converge to some $f\in C[a,b]$, this $f$ satisfies $f=u+Kf$, and it is the only solution.

Three ideas in that proof are the subject of Section 1.2 and of most of what follows. A norm measures the size of a function with one number. Completeness guarantees that a Cauchy sequence of functions has a limit inside the space. A bounded linear operator such as $K$ passes through limits, which is what lets us conclude $f=u+Kf$ from $f_{n+1}=u+Kf_n$.

The goal further on is the equation $(I-K)f=u$ for operators $K$ that are not so obliging. For the $K$ above, $I-K$ is always invertible. Integrate over all of $[a,b]$ instead and it may not be. Operators of the form $I-K$ with $K$ compact are the first examples of Fredholm operators: they can fail to be invertible, but only in a finite-dimensional way. The switch in the Geometry panel is a first look.

3Experiments

Each button sets up the laboratory above and takes you back to it.

The formula, tested

Equation (1.1) with g = cos(x − a). The integral formula and an ODE solver agree. The solution grows without bound even though g does not: the forcing is in step with u.

A series you can sum by hand

With σ ≡ 1 the equation is f″ = 0, so f ≡ 1. Yet u = cos(x − a). Step n from 0 upward and watch cosine, plus corrections, flatten into a constant.

Worse before better

On a long interval M(b − a) = 12. The early terms grow and fn is nowhere near f. Then n! takes over. Find the n at which the band first becomes thinner than the curve.

The matrix runs out of room

With m = 8, raise the power j. Each multiplication pushes the entries one more step below the diagonal. At j = 8 nothing is left. Then raise m and try again.

When I − K fails

Integrate over all of [a, b] with σ₀ = cos 2(x − a). Drag λ toward the point where the crimson determinant crosses zero and watch f. Then go back to a ≤ t ≤ x with the same λ.

A solution the series cannot reach

Still over all of [a, b], now with λ = 1.5. The determinant is far from zero and f exists, but the terms ‖Kju‖ grow forever. Iteration is one way to invert I − K, not the only way.

4What the picture proves, and what it does not

What you may trust

  • For equation (1.1) the integral formula and the ODE solver are independent computations. Their agreement is evidence for the first statement, one $g$ at a time. The proof is two differentiations.
  • The bound $(M(b-a))^n/n!$ is a theorem, proved by induction. The gray bars must lie under the dashed line for every $\sigma$ you can draw, and they do.
  • The matrix of $K$ has zeros on and above the diagonal because $t\le x$ and $\sin(x-x)=0$. So $I-K_m$ is triangular with ones on the diagonal, its determinant is $1$, and the sampled problem is solvable for every $\sigma$. This is a fact about matrices and it is exact.
  • The band around $f$ really is the ball $\{h:\|h-f\|\le\varepsilon\}$ of $C[a,b]$. Uniform convergence means the band shrinks to the graph.

What the screen cannot show

  • A strictly triangular $m\times m$ matrix satisfies $K_m^m=0$, so on the screen the series stops after $m$ terms. The operator $K$ on $C[a,b]$ is not nilpotent: $K^n\ne0$ for every $n$. What survives the passage to infinitely many dimensions is the estimate with $n!$, not the vanishing.
  • There is no determinant of $I-K$ on $C[a,b]$. The plotted curve belongs to a large matrix. Saying what replaces the determinant is the work of the Fredholm theory.
  • A Cauchy sequence needs somewhere to converge. On the screen every sequence of columns of numbers converges. That the limit of the $f_n$ is again a continuous function is the completeness of $C[a,b]$, and it needs proof.
  • Both reference curves are approximations. The error $\|f-f_n\|$ is measured against the limit of the sampled iteration, so it shows the truncation of the series and nothing else. The ODE solver is an independent check: compare the last two readouts under the matrix. On a long interval they differ in the third decimal, because small quadrature errors are amplified by $(I-K)^{-1}$.
  • In the look ahead, the matrix is singular at one value of $\lambda$ and invertible at all others. Whether an operator on an infinite-dimensional space behaves so tamely is exactly what it means to be Fredholm. The picture suggests the question and does not answer it.

5Guided questions

  1. Differentiate $f(x)=\cos(x-a)+\int_a^x\sin(x-t)g(t)\,dt$ twice and verify (1.1) and (1.2). At which step do you use $\sin(x-x)=0$, and at which step $\cos(x-x)=1$? Which feature of the matrix in the Coordinates panel records the first fact?
  2. Prove $|K^nh(x)|\le M^n(x-a)^n\|h\|/n!$ by induction on $n$. Set up "Worse before better" and find, from the inequality alone, an $n$ that guarantees $\|f-f_n\|\lt10^{-6}$. Compare with the smallest $n$ that works in the laboratory. Why is the bound so pessimistic here?
  3. Show that a strictly lower triangular $m\times m$ matrix $N$ satisfies $N^m=0$ and $(I-N)^{-1}=I+N+\dots+N^{m-1}$. Then take $\sigma\equiv1$ and compute $K1$ and $K^21$ by hand to see that $K$ itself is not nilpotent. Show that nevertheless $\|K^n\|^{1/n}\to0$. What does this say about solving $f=u+\lambda Kf$ for large $\lambda$?
  4. Now let $Kh(x)=\int_a^b\sin(x-t)\sigma(t)h(t)\,dt$. Use $\sin(x-t)=\sin x\cos t-\cos x\sin t$ to show that every $Kh$ is a combination of $\sin x$ and $\cos x$, and reduce $f=u+\lambda Kf$ to two linear equations in two unknowns with determinant $1+\lambda^2(QR-P^2)$, where $P=\int\sigma\sin t\cos t$, $Q=\int\sigma\cos^2t$, $R=\int\sigma\sin^2t$. Prove that if $\sigma\ge0$ the equation is uniquely solvable for every real $\lambda$. For $\sigma=\cos2(x-a)$, find in the laboratory the $\lambda$ at which solvability fails. At that $\lambda$, how many independent solutions does $f=\lambda Kf$ have, and how many conditions must $u$ satisfy for $f=u+\lambda Kf$ to be solvable? Keep your two numbers. Their difference is the index.
