The central puzzle

Why one function has three series

A Fourier series is periodic, so before you can expand anything you must say what the function does everywhere — and f was only ever handed to you on [0, L].

Choosing sines commits you to the odd extension, f(−x) = −f(x). Choosing cosines commits you to the even one, f(−x) = f(x). You did not change f; you chose how to continue it past the left edge, and each choice has its own coefficients.

Tick overlay all three series. Inside the shaded window the three curves lie on top of one another — every one of them is converging to the same f. To the left of 0 they split apart, because there they are drawing three different functions.

Reading the coefficient panel

Smoothness sets the decay rate

Choose Sawtooth — x and compare the two half-range tabs, watching the coefficient bars.

The sine series has bn ~ 2/(nπ): the odd extension of x is x, whose periodic copy jumps from 1 down to −1 at every edge. Coefficients die off like 1/n, and SN rings near x = L no matter how large N gets.

The cosine series gives the even extension |x| — continuous, merely kinked. Its coefficients fall like 1/n² and the approximation is visibly better with the same N. Turn on the logarithmic axis and the two slopes are unmistakable.

Worth doing in front of a class

Four demonstrations

The exact one-term series. Square wave, cosine tab. On [0, L] the function is just the constant 1, so a0/2 = 1 and every other coefficient vanishes: N = 1 is already exact. The sine tab on the identical data needs infinitely many terms.

Gibbs. Square wave, sine tab, then push N to 60. The overshoot near the jump narrows but never shrinks — watch the error panel, not the main plot.

Smoothness pays. The smooth bump reaches plotting accuracy in a handful of terms.
