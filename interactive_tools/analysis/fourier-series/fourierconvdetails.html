Cesàro Means, Fejér's Kernel, and Approximate Identities

Averaging the partial sums repairs everything that went wrong in Lecture 3 — and the proof is one ε-argument with two knobs.
A — The construction, and the algebra that closes it on (−π, π), period 2π
definition

Write sn for the symmetric partial sums of the Fourier series of f. The (first) Cesàro means are their arithmetic averages:

σn = (1/n)( s0 + s1 + ⋯ + sn−1 ),    n = 1, 2, …

Substituting ck = (1/2π)∫f(t)e−iktdt into sn and exchanging the finite sum with the integral gives a convolution, and averaging preserves that form:

σn(x) = (1/2π) ∫−ππ f(t) Kn(x−t) dt  =  (1/2π) ∫−ππ f(x−t) Kn(t) dt

where Kn is the n-th Cesàro mean of Σk=−∞∞ eikx. The second form uses only the periodicity of the extension of f.

closing the kernel in closed form
step 1 · telescope
(n+1)Kn+1(x) − nKn(x) = Σk=−nn eikx = Dn(x)
step 2 · sum the two geometric series
Dn(x) = (1−ei(n+1)x)/(1−eix) + (1−e−i(n+1)x)/(1−e−ix) − 1 = [ cos nx − cos(n+1)x ] / (1 − cos x)
step 3 · solve the recursion from K₁ = 1
Kn(x) = (1/n) · (1 − cos nx)/(1 − cos x)  =  (1/n) [ sin(nx/2) / sin(x/2) ]²

The last equality is just 1 − cos θ = 2 sin²(θ/2) — and it is the whole reason the theory works, because it exhibits Kn as a square. Positivity is not an accident of this kernel; it is visible in the formula.

B — The three properties: an approximate identity measured values are numerical illustration

Everything below is proved from these three facts alone. Any integrable sequence satisfying them is called an approximate identity for L¹ (or a positive kernel). Evenness is never used — the one-sided variant K′n = 2Kn on [0,π], zero on [−π,0), satisfies all three just as well, and the panel below measures it alongside.

Property (iii) follows from an inequality worth keeping: if 0 < δ ≤ |x| ≤ π then sin²(x/2) ≥ sin²(δ/2), hence

| Kn(x) | ≤ 1 / ( n sin²(δ/2) ),    δ ≤ |x| ≤ π.

The right panel of chart 2 draws this bound against the true supremum, so you can see how much the estimate gives away — and that both go to zero in n with δ held fixed, which is all the proof asks of them.

3 — The proof of the uniform theorem, as a picture two knobs, in this order: δ first, then n

Because (1/2π)∫Kn = 1 we may subtract f inside the integral, then cut the domain at |t| = δ:

σn(x) − f(x) = (1/2π)∫[ f(x−t) − f(x) ] Kn(t) dt = (1/2π)∫|t|≤δ ⋯ + (1/2π)∫|t|≥δ ⋯
the estimate
|σn(x) − f(x)|  ≤  sup|t|≤δ|f(x−t) − f(x)|  +  2‖f‖∞ · sup|t|≥δKn(t)

The first piece needs both (i) and (ii): non-negativity lets us pull the sup out, and total mass one stops it growing. The second needs only (iii).

Shrink δ and the crimson term falls (continuity), but the bronze term rises, since a narrower tail means a larger supremum to beat. Then hold δ and raise n: bronze collapses on its own. That asymmetry is why the quantifiers come in the order they do.

4 — Where the two theorems part company same proof, different modulus of continuity

For f ∈ Lp the duality step — pair with g ∈ Lq, apply Hölder inside, then choose g by Hahn–Banach so the pairing recovers the norm — yields the same shape of inequality with ‖ft − f‖p in place of a pointwise oscillation, where ft(x) = f(x−t):

‖σn − f‖p ≤ (1/2π)∫ ‖ft − f‖p Kn(t) dt ≤ sup|t|≤δ‖ft−f‖p + 2‖f‖p·sup|t|≥δKn

So the two theorems are one theorem applied to two moduli. The entire difference is whether ω(δ) → 0 as δ → 0: translation is continuous in the Lp-norm for every f ∈ Lp, but continuous in the sup-norm only when the periodic extension of f is continuous. The left chart is that sentence.

Why the repair works at all

Positivity forbids overshoot

Since Kn ≥ 0 and (1/2π)∫Kn = 1, the value σn(x) is a weighted average of the values of f. An average cannot exceed the largest value being averaged, so

inf f ≤ σn(x) ≤ sup f

for every n and every x. No Gibbs overshoot is possible — not reduced, impossible. Chart 1 shades that range; switch between the two summation methods and watch only sn leave it.

Dirichlet's kernel is not a square and does change sign. Its L¹ norms, the Lebesgue constants, grow like (4/π²)log n — shown in chart 2 — and that unbounded growth is precisely the room in which Gibbs lives.

The logical shape

Why δ must be chosen first

The bound is ω(δ) + 2‖f‖·sup|t|≥δKn. The two terms pull opposite ways in δ, and only one of them can be attacked with n.

So the argument runs: given ε, use continuity to pick δ with ω(δ) < ε/2 — this uses nothing about the kernel. Then, with δ frozen, use (iii) to pick N making the second term < ε/2 for all n ≥ N.

Reversing the order fails: fixing n first leaves the bronze term at the mercy of a δ you have not chosen, and it diverges as δ → 0. In chart 5 the frontier's shape is this fact — the certificate region opens to the right, never upward from δ = 0.

What the specimens are for

Four ways to stress the hypotheses

sgn(x) — bounded but with a jump. ωp(δ) = 2(δ/π)1/p → 0, so Lp convergence holds; ω∞(δ) ≡ 2, so uniform convergence has no chance. The two certificate maps disagree, exactly as advertised.

x/π — continuous on the open interval, yet f(−π) ≠ f(π). The periodic extension jumps, and ω∞ is again constant. This is why the theorem's hypothesis is f(−π) = f(π) and not merely continuity on (−π, π).

|x|−1/3 — unbounded, so ‖f‖∞ = ∞ and the uniform bound is vacuous, while ‖f‖p is finite exactly for p < 3. Set p = 3 or 4 and the Lp statement withdraws too: f is no longer in the space.

triangle and ecos x — Lipschitz and analytic. Both moduli vanish linearly; both theorems apply; the certificate regions agree.

What is computed and what is proved

Every number on this page is measured — quadrature on a graded mesh, suprema over finite grids, floating point throughout. It illustrates the theorems; it establishes nothing.

Specifically: each supremum is a maximum over finitely many samples, hence a lower bound for the true one, so both ω curves and the measured errors sit slightly low. The integrals carry quadrature error of roughly 1e−9, larger for |x|−1/3 where the integrand is unbounded. Curves that flatten near those levels have found the arithmetic's floor, not a mathematical limit.

The certificate map is a sufficient condition evaluated on a 64 × 64 lattice: an unshaded cell means this bound failed there, not that σn is far from f. Emptiness of the shaded set at a given ε is suggestive, never conclusive — the honest statement is that ω∞(δ) is provably constant for these jump specimens, and the picture agrees.

The bound drawn is the estimate the proof actually uses, tail factor and all, and it is lossy: it needs n of order 1/(δ²ε), so certificates appear only at n around 105 even for the analytic specimen, while the true error is already negligible by n = 20. That gap between what the estimate guarantees and what happens is normal, and it is why the n axis of chart 5 spans nine decades rather than the 200 you can plot.
