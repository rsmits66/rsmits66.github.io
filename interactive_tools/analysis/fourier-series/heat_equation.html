Heat Conduction in a Rod

Separation of variables gives a sum of modes, each decaying at its own rate. The boundary conditions decide what the modes are.
The problem, and what separation of variables produces rod of length 1, temperature u(x, t)
ut = α uxx + Q(x),    0 < x < 1,   u(x, 0) = f(x)

Split off the steady state: write u = v(x) + w(x, t), where v solves αv″ + Q = 0 with the actual boundary data. Then w satisfies the same equation with no source and homogeneous boundary conditions, and separating w = φ(x)T(t) forces

−φ″ = λφ   and   T′ = −αλT,   so   w = Σ cn φn(x) e−αλnt

Every mode decays, and it decays at a rate αλn proportional to its eigenvalue. Since λn grows like n², the wiggly modes vanish enormously faster than the smooth ones — which is why a rough initial profile becomes smooth almost at once, and why the last thing you see is always the first mode.

fixed temperature · Dirichlet

u = T at the end

φ(0) = 0  ⟹  φn = sin nπx, λn = (nπ)²
The end is clamped to a reservoir. Heat leaves freely, so every mode decays and the steady state is set entirely by the two end temperatures.
insulated · Neumann

ux = 0 at the end

φ′(0) = 0  ⟹  φn = cos nπx, λn = (nπ)²
No heat crosses the end. Note λ = 0 is now an eigenvalue, with φ ≡ 1: the mean temperature has nothing to decay towards, so it is conserved.
surface heat transfer · Robin

−k ux = h(u − T∞)

φ′(1) + Bi·φ(1) = 0  ⟹  tan μ = −μ/Bi
Newton cooling into a surrounding fluid. The eigenvalues are no longer multiples of π — they solve a transcendental equation, and they slide continuously from the insulated values (Bi → 0) to the fixed-temperature ones (Bi → ∞).

The first thing to try

Watch n² do the work

Choose hot left half with both ends fixed at 0 and press play. The corner at the middle rounds off almost immediately, then the whole profile settles into a single arch before sinking to zero.

That sequence is the factor e−αλnt with λn = (nπ)². Mode 5 decays 25 times faster than mode 1, mode 10 a hundred times faster. Whatever the initial data, after a short while only the first mode is left — so the late-time shape is a property of the boundary conditions, not of f.

Use isolate mode to pull out one term, and the Modes tab to watch the whole spectrum of amplitudes collapse from the right.

The three boundary conditions

Insulate one end and watch

With both ends fixed at 0, all the heat eventually leaves and u → 0. Set the left end to insulated and the first eigenvalue drops from π² to (π/2)² — a quarter of the decay rate, so the rod holds its heat four times longer.

Insulate both ends and λ = 0 appears with φ ≡ 1. Nothing can leave, so the mean temperature is exactly conserved and the rod flattens out to its own average rather than to zero. Check it against the mean readout.

Now set an end to convective and sweep the Biot number. At Bi ≈ 0.01 the eigenvalues sit almost on the insulated values; by Bi ≈ 100 they have slid onto the fixed-temperature ones. Robin is the one-parameter family joining the other two.

Source terms

When there is no steady state

Turn on a uniform source with both ends fixed: v(x) becomes a parabola, and the solution climbs to it and stops. The source is balanced by conduction out of the ends.

Now insulate both ends with the source still on. Heat is being created and none can escape, so no steady state exists — the banner says so, and the whole rod rises without limit at rate ∫Q dx while the shape still settles down.

That is the one case where u = v + w has to be replaced by u = Gt + p(x) + w. The solvability condition for the steady problem is exactly ∫Q = 0, which is a statement about conservation, not about analysis.

The energy tab

Why the solution is unique

Suppose two solutions had the same source, boundary data and initial data. Their difference w solves the problem with everything homogeneous and w(x,0) = 0. Put E(t) = ½∫₀¹w² dx and differentiate:

E′ = α[w wx]₀¹ − α∫(wx)²

Each boundary condition kills or signs the bracket — zero for fixed and insulated ends, and −Bi·w² at a convective one. So E′ ≤ 0 always. With E(0) = 0 and E ≥ 0, E ≡ 0, so w ≡ 0.

The tab plots E(t) for the transient actually on screen and checks the identity term by term. Numbers there are computed, not proved: they illustrate the inequality that does the work.
