From Fourier Series to Fourier Integrals

Hold the function still, let the interval grow, and watch a sum turn into an integral.
The passage, in four steps six functions whose transforms are known exactly

Put a localized f on the interval (−L, L) and expand it as a Fourier series. The available frequencies are ωn = nπ/L, spaced Δω = π/L apart, and the coefficients are

cn = (1/2L) ∫−LL f(x) e−iωnx dx

As L grows the spacing shrinks and each individual coefficient shrinks with it — which is why cn is the wrong thing to plot. Multiply by 2L and the L disappears:

2L cn = ∫−LL f e−iωnx dx  ⟶  f̂(ω) = ∫−∞∞ f e−iωx dx

The two differ by exactly the part of f lying outside (−L, L). So for a function of compact support the agreement is not approximate: it becomes exact the moment L clears the support.

step one

The spacing closes up

Δω = π/L → 0
Double L and you double the number of available frequencies below any fixed ceiling. The stems in the lower panel crowd together while their envelope stays put.
step two

The sum is a Riemann sum

Σ cneiωnx = (1/2π) Σ f̂(ωn) eiωnx Δω
Because 1/2L = Δω/2π exactly, the partial sum is a Riemann sum for the inversion integral. There is one limit here, not two.
step three

Continuous superposition

f(x) = (1/2π)∫ f̂(ω)eiωxdω
In the limit f is built not from countably many harmonics with definite amplitudes but from a continuum, each contributing infinitesimally. f̂ is a density in ω, not an amplitude.
step four

Heat becomes multiplication

û(ω, t) = f̂(ω) e−αω²t
On an unbounded medium there are no boundaries to select a spectrum, so every ω is allowed — and each simply decays at its own rate. High frequencies go first, which is the same statement as the profile spreading like √t.

The demonstration to run first

Compact support makes it exact

Start with the tent, whose support is |x| < 1.5, and press Grow L. Watch two things at once: in the lower panel the stems close up while their envelope stands still, and in panel 2 the discrepancy between 2Lcn and f̂(ωn) collapses to the arithmetic floor as soon as L passes 1.5.

That is not convergence in the usual sense. The two quantities differ by ∫|x|>L f e−iωxdx, and once L clears the support that integral is zero. The transform is being sampled, not approximated.

Now switch to the Gaussian or the exponential. There is always a tail, so the discrepancy only shrinks — fast for the Gaussian, slowly for the exponential, whose tail is much fatter. Panel 2 shows the difference.

What the copies are doing

Two errors, not one

A Fourier series on (−L, L) cannot represent a function that is not periodic; what it represents is the periodized f, the sum of copies at spacing 2L. Those copies are drawn faintly in the upper panel.

So the reconstruction misses f for two separate reasons: the copies leak into the window, and the frequency window ω ≤ ωmax is finite. Growing L fixes the first; raising ωmax fixes the second. Panel 2 reports them apart.

Keep ωmax fixed and grow L: the number of terms grows in proportion, because N = ωmaxL/π. The same frequency content now needs more terms — which is exactly the sense in which the sum is becoming an integral.

Heat on the half-line

The old odd and even trick, now continuous

With u(0,t) = 0 the right tool is the sine transform, and with u′(0,t) = 0 the cosine transform. These are the half-range sine and cosine series of Lecture 1, with the sum replaced by an integral.

The reflected image drawn on x < 0 makes it plain. The sine transform is solving the whole-line problem for the odd extension, so the boundary stays pinned at zero by antisymmetry; the cosine transform uses the even extension, so no flux crosses the boundary by symmetry.

Equivalently u = ∫[G(x−y) ∓ G(x+y)]f(y)dy, an image source of opposite sign for the fixed end and the same sign for the insulated one. Panel 2 checks the transform answer against that formula — two genuinely independent routes.

Worth knowing before quoting a number

Where the numbers come from

The continuous transforms here are closed form — a Gaussian, a sinc, a sinc squared, a Lorentzian — so the curve the coefficients are compared against carries no error. Only the coefficients are computed, by Simpson's rule on a mesh split at every break in f, which is what lets the compact-support demonstration land near 1e−10 rather than 1e−4.

The inversion integral is cut at a finite ω. That is harmless for the Gaussian, whose transform decays like a Gaussian, but the rectangle's decays only like 1/ω, so its reconstruction rings and its accuracy is limited by the truncation rather than the arithmetic. That is honest behaviour of the transform, not a defect of the method.

At t = 0 exactly, the heat panel performs the full inversion with no damping, so it shows the same ringing — and the ∫u dx reading inherits it, which for the rectangle is a couple of percent out. Step t off zero and the factor e−αω²t cuts the tail: the integral then lands on the exact conserved mass to eleven digits for the Gaussian.

That integral is taken over the visible window, not the whole line, so at late times it starts to fall — not because heat is being lost but because the profile has grown wider than the frame. Widen the x view and the reading holds still again. Conservation on an unbounded domain is not something a bounded measurement can confirm.
