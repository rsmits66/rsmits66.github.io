Rectangular Membranes and Fourier Series in Two Variables

Two indices instead of one — and the frequencies stop being multiples of anything.
Product eigenfunctions, and the two clocks that run on them membrane clamped on all four edges, a = 1

Separating twice turns the two-dimensional eigenvalue problem into two one-dimensional ones. Each is the problem from Lecture 1, so the answers simply multiply:

φmn(x, y) = (2/√(ab)) sin(mπx/a) sin(nπy/b),    λmn = (mπ/a)² + (nπ/b)²

Those same product functions serve both evolutions. Only the time factor differs, and that single difference is the whole distinction between a drum and a cooling plate:

wave: cos(ωmnt), ωmn = c√λmn    versus    heat: e−αλmnt
nodal lines

A grid, for a single mode

x = ka/m,   y = lb/n
The zero set of a product is where either factor vanishes, so mode (m, n) has m−1 vertical and n−1 horizontal nodal lines. Sand on a vibrating plate collects there — that is what Chladni was photographing.
repeated frequencies

And then the grid bends

λmn = λnm on a square
When two modes share a frequency, every combination of them is also an eigenfunction at that frequency — and its nodal set is generally curved. The mixing control below sweeps through a whole family of shapes that all ring at one pitch.
the aspect ratio matters

Degeneracy needs b² rational

b² = (q²−n²)/(m²−p²)
The right-hand side is a ratio of integers, so a coincidence can only happen when b² is rational — and then it never happens at all. Note the trap: b = √2 looks irrational but b² = 2, so that rectangle is full of coincidences. Take b⁴ = 2 instead and there are none, for any m, n at all.
why drums have no pitch

Incommensurate overtones

ω ∝ √(m² + n²/b²)
On a string the overtones were 1 : 2 : 3. Here they are square roots. The diagonal modes are the exception — ωkk/ω11 = k exactly, whatever b — but ω21/ω11 = √(5/2) and its like are irrational, so the motion never repeats. A struck membrane makes a thud, not a note.

The first thing to try

Nodal grids, then curves

Set (m, n) = (3, 2) and press play. The shape never moves: it is a standing wave, and its two vertical and one horizontal nodal lines stay exactly where they are while everything between them pumps up and down.

Now set (2, 1) on a square and drag the mix control from 0° to 90°. Both (2, 1) and (1, 2) ring at the same frequency, so every blend is a legitimate mode — and the nodal line sweeps from vertical, through a diagonal, to horizontal. At 45° the nodal set is a straight diagonal; nearby it bows into a curve.

Then press b⁴ = 2. The partner readout goes empty and stays empty: with b² = √2 irrational, b² = (q²−n²)/(m²−p²) can never hold, so no two modes anywhere share a frequency and every nodal pattern is forced to be a grid.

Worth trying the near miss too. Press b = √2, which looks irrational — yet b² = 2 is a perfectly good rational number, and that rectangle turns out to have nine degenerate pairs among the first 8 × 8 modes, including (1,5) with (3,3). What matters is b², not b.

Reading the spectrum grid

Coincidences are visible

Each cell of the grid is a mode, shaded by its frequency. Cells ringed in bronze share their frequency with at least one other cell — and on a square there are a lot of them, because λmn = λnm always.

Look for the coincidences that are not just a swap. On the square, (1, 7) and (5, 5) both give 1 + 49 = 25 + 25 = 50, so they ring together even though neither is a reflection of the other. That is a three-fold degeneracy once you count (7, 1).

Slide the aspect ratio slowly and watch the rings appear and vanish. Each one is a value of b² at which some ratio (q²−n²)/(m²−p²) is hit exactly, so the coincidences are dense in b but each individual one is fragile.

The series tab

One set of modes, two clocks

The double Fourier coefficients are the same object either way: Amn = ⟨f, φmn⟩. What you do with them next is a choice.

The two right-hand panels run from identical coefficients. Under cos ωmnt the surface sloshes and keeps its roughness for ever, because every mode holds its amplitude. Under e−αλmnt the same surface goes smooth almost at once, because a mode with λ nine times larger dies nine times faster.

Watch the probe traces in panel 3: oscillation that never quite repeats, against a clean monotone decay. Same spatial modes, entirely different fates.

The wave trace never closes because the frequencies are incommensurate. The one family that does line up is the diagonal, ωkk = k·ω11 exactly — visible in panel 3 as the only bars touching the integer lines.

Worth being careful about

Truncation, and what the nodal lines mean

The coefficient sum is cut at M × N terms, so a discontinuous initial surface — the plateau, say — rings at its edges exactly as a one-dimensional series would. Raise M and the ringing narrows without shrinking; that is still Gibbs, now in two dimensions.

Nodal lines are drawn as the zero contour of the field at the current instant. For one mode, or for any blend of modes sharing a frequency, they are genuinely stationary. For a combination of different frequencies they move, and what you are watching is a moving zero set rather than a nodal pattern. The readout says which case you are in.

Frequencies, energies and errors here are computed on a finite grid in floating point. They illustrate the statements above; they do not establish them.
