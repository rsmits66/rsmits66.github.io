The Fourier Convergence Laboratory

Three kinds of convergence, three kinds of function, and one constant that refuses to go away.
The theorem, and the three senses of "converges" period 2, harmonics cos nπx and sin nπx

Fourier convergence theorem. Let f be periodic with period 2L and let f and f′ be piecewise continuous on [−L, L]. Then for every x the Fourier series converges, and its sum is

S(x) = ½ [ f(x⁻) + f(x⁺) ]

— the average of the two one-sided limits. Where f is continuous that is just f(x); at a jump it is the midpoint, which the series hits regardless of how f was defined there.

weakest of the three

Pointwise

SN(x₀) → S(x₀)  for each fixed x₀
Fix the point first, then send N → ∞. How fast may depend on the point, and near a jump it depends on it badly. This is what the theorem above delivers.
strongest of the three

Uniform

supx∈I |f(x) − SN(x)| → 0
One N must work for every point of I at once. A jump inside I destroys this: the sup cannot fall below half the jump. Exclude the jump and it returns.
the forgiving one

Mean-square

∫−LL |f − SN|² dx → 0
Equals the leftover tail energy Σn>N(aₙ²+bₙ²)·L, so it dies for every square-integrable f — jumps and all. Blind to what happens at single points.

The experiment to run first

Three specimens, one N

Set N = 12 and step through the specimens without touching anything else. The smooth one is already indistinguishable from f. The corner is close everywhere but slightly rounded at the kink. The jump rings.

That ordering is the convergence theorem doing its work: the smoother f is, the faster its coefficients decay — geometrically, then 1/n², then 1/n — and the coefficient decay is what controls how fast SN closes in.

Then press go to the endpoint x = 1 with the sawtooth. Nothing is wrong with f at x = 1; the trouble is that the periodic extension jumps there, from 1 down to −1. Endpoint misbehaviour is almost always this in disguise.

Reading panel 2

Why the curves separate

For a jump specimen the crimson mean-square error slides steadily to zero while the charcoal sup-norm flattens onto a horizontal barrier. Same series, same N, two verdicts: mean-square convergence yes, uniform convergence no.

The barrier sits at J/2, half the jump. SN is continuous and must pass through the midpoint, so just beside the jump it is about J/2 away from f. No N can help.

Now zoom into a smooth stretch away from every jump. The bronze curve — the sup over just the visible window — peels off and heads to zero. Uniform convergence holds on any closed interval avoiding the jumps, and that is exactly what the bronze curve is measuring.

Two numbers people mix up

J/2 is not the Gibbs constant

The barrier is J/2: the worst error near a jump, caused by SN being continuous where f is not.

The Gibbs overshoot is how far SN shoots past f's own value on the far side — it tends to 0.0894898·J, from the Wilbraham–Gibbs constant Si(π)/π − ½. Panel 1 draws those levels; panel 1's stat strip measures the overshoot for you.

Raise N and watch: the ripple narrows toward the jump but its height holds at roughly 0.0895 J. It does not shrink — it retreats. That is the whole phenomenon.

What these numbers can and cannot show

Every figure here is measured, on a finite grid, in floating point. Measurements illustrate theorems; they do not establish them.

A supremum computed over finitely many sample points is a lower bound for the true supremum — the real peak generally falls between samples, so the charcoal and bronze curves sit slightly low, especially at large N where the ripple is narrow.

To keep that honest where it matters most, the sampling is deliberately clustered onto each jump. Without it the measured sup would sag as N grew and imitate slow uniform convergence — an artefact of the grid, not a fact about the series.

The coefficients come from Simpson quadrature and the mean-square error is recovered from them by Parseval, so both carry quadrature error, here around 1e−9. A curve flattening onto its own floor — about 1e−9 for the jump specimens, near machine precision for the smooth one — has reached the limit of this arithmetic, not a limit of the mathematics. And no finite N can separate "tends to 0.0895 J" from "tends to something extremely close to it"; for that you need the proof, not the picture.
