Laplace and Poisson Equations in a Rectangle

No time variable at all — the boundary decides everything, all at once.
The problem, and the four pieces it breaks into rectangle 0 < x < a, 0 < y < b
∇²u + F(x, y) = 0   inside,    u given (or un = 0) on each side

Separating u = X(x)Y(y) in Laplace's equation gives X″/X = −Y″/Y = −μ²: one factor oscillates and the other grows. So the transverse factor is a hyperbolic function rather than a decaying exponential, and its job is to carry the data from the side where it lives across to the far side, where it must vanish.

u = Σ cn · Xn(x) · sinh μn(b − y) / sinh μnb

That factor is 1 on the data edge and 0 on the opposite edge, so a single side's data can be handled on its own. Four sides, four such problems, and superposition puts them back together. The strip below the main picture shows exactly that sum being formed.

Dirichlet · all four sides given

Unique, and bounded by the data

min∂ u ≤ u(x,y) ≤ max∂ u
The maximum principle: a harmonic function takes its extremes on the boundary and nowhere else. Every value inside is an average of its neighbours, so nothing can bulge. The readout checks it for you.
mixed · insulate a side

The basis changes shape

ux = 0 at x = 0  ⟹  cos, not sin
An insulated side carries no data at all; it changes which eigenfunctions the other sides must be expanded in. Insulate both x-sides and λ = 0 joins the x-basis, giving a term linear in y.
Poisson · a source inside

The principle breaks

∇²u = −F, F > 0 ⟹ u may peak inside
Heat made in the interior has to pile up somewhere. With a source the maximum can sit strictly inside, which is the sharpest way to see that interior forcing is not boundary forcing.
pure Neumann · all sides insulated

Compatibility, then a free constant

∬ F dA = 0  required;   u + C also solves
With no side able to pass heat, the source must produce exactly none on balance or there is no steady state. And if it does balance, u is determined only up to an additive constant — the same λ = 0 obstruction as in Lecture 5.

The first thing to try

One side at a time

Press One hot side: the bottom is held at 1, the other three at 0. The heat leaks away from the hot edge and the isotherms bow outward. Note how fast it dies: the sinh ratio falls off roughly like e−nπy/a, so the wiggly parts of the data are felt only in a thin layer near their own edge. A rough boundary profile looks smooth a short way inside.

Now press Two sides and watch the decomposition strip. Each panel is a complete solution in its own right; the last panel is their sum. That is all superposition is, and it works because both the equation and the boundary conditions are linear.

Where two hot sides meet a cold one, the data is discontinuous at the corner. The series rings there — genuine Gibbs, from genuinely discontinuous data.

The maximum principle

Nothing bulges

With F = 0, panel 3 shows two cross-sections of u inside the bronze band running between the smallest and largest boundary values. The curves never leave it, and touch the edges only at the boundary itself.

The reason is the mean-value property: u at any interior point is the average of u over any small circle around it. An average cannot exceed everything it averages, so an interior maximum would force u to be constant nearby — and hence constant everywhere.

This is what makes the Dirichlet problem well behaved. Uniqueness follows at once: two solutions differ by a harmonic function that is zero on the boundary, so its max and min are both 0.

Interior source versus boundary data

Where the principle fails

Press Poisson blob: all four sides are held at 0, yet u is strictly positive inside. Every boundary value is 0, so max∂u = 0, and the interior maximum is plainly above it. The readout flags the violation.

No contradiction — the principle was only ever a statement about harmonic functions. With ∇²u = −F and F > 0, u is superharmonic: its average over a small circle is less than the centre value, which is precisely permission to have an interior peak.

Flip the source sign and the peak becomes a pit. The correct general statement keeps a one-sided bound: with F ≥ 0 the minimum is still on the boundary.

Pure Neumann

Two things go wrong at once

Press Pure Neumann: every side insulated, a uniform source inside. The banner reports no solution, and the reason is conservation, not analysis. Integrating ∇²u = −F over the rectangle and using the divergence theorem gives

∮ un ds = −∬ F dA

With every side insulated the left side is 0, so ∬F must vanish. In the series this is the λ00 = 0 mode: its coefficient would need dividing by zero. Press Make F compatible — which just subtracts the mean — or choose the dipole source, and a solution appears.

Even then u is fixed only up to a constant: λ00 = 0 means constants are homogeneous solutions. Switch fix constant by and the whole field shifts rigidly while every contour keeps its shape.
