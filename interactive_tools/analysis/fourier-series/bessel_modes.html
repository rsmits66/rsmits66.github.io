Bessel Functions, Cylinders, and Circular Membranes

The same separation of variables — but in polar coordinates the radial equation is no longer constant-coefficient.
Why Bessel functions are unavoidable here unit disk, a = 1

In polar coordinates the Laplacian carries the geometry with it:

∇²u = urr + (1/r) ur + (1/r²) uθθ

Separating u = R(r)Θ(θ), periodicity in θ forces Θ = cos nθ or sin nθ with n a whole number — that part is still trigonometric. What is left for R is not:

r²R″ + rR′ + (λr² − n²)R = 0

Those 1/r and n²/r² terms are the price of the circle, and they make the equation non-constant-coefficient. Its bounded solution is Jn(√λ r). Bessel functions are not an exotic import; they are what sin and cos become when the domain is round.

the boundary picks the zeros

A clamped rim

Jn(√λ) = 0 ⟹ √λ = jn,m
The admissible radial parameters are exactly the zeros of Jn. Nothing else is available. This is the circular analogue of √λ = nπ/L, with a far less tidy list of numbers.
degeneracy, now systematic

Every n ≥ 1 is doubled

cos nθ and sin nθ share λnm
On the rectangle degeneracy was an accident of the aspect ratio. Here it is forced by rotational symmetry, for every mode with angular structure — and mixing the pair simply rotates the pattern rather than reshaping it.
the weight, explained at last

r is the area element

∬ f g dA = ∬ f g r dr dθ
Lecture 9 introduced a weight in the inner product as a feature of Sturm–Liouville form. Here you can see where it comes from: it is simply dA = r dr dθ. Orthogonality was always geometric.

The first thing to try

Read the pattern off the indices

Mode (n, m) has exactly n nodal diameters and m − 1 nodal circles. Set n = 2, m = 3 and count them on the disk: two diameters crossing at the centre, two interior circles. The circles sit at r = jn,k/jn,m — ratios of Bessel zeros, so they are not evenly spaced, and they bunch slightly toward the rim.

Then look at the left panel. The radial profile is literally the piece of Jn between 0 and its m-th zero, stretched to fit the radius. The boundary condition is doing nothing more than choosing which zero lands on the rim.

Press play. The whole pattern stands still and only breathes — a standing wave, exactly as on the string and the rectangle.

Degeneracy, and why it rotates

Symmetry does it for free

For any n ≥ 1, cos nθ and sin nθ belong to the same λ, so the eigenspace is two-dimensional for every such mode. Drag rotate the degenerate pair: the combination is cos(nθ − φ), so the nodal diameters simply turn.

Compare Lecture 8. On the rectangle, mixing two degenerate modes bent the nodal lines into curves, because the two partners were genuinely different shapes. Here the partners are the same shape rotated, so the family is a rotation — which is what rotational symmetry of the domain guarantees.

Note also that n = 0 is not degenerate: there is no sin 0θ. The purely radial modes are the only simple ones on a disk.

The heat tab

Two surface conditions, two equations

A long cylinder cooling radially has admissible parameters fixed by its surface. Clamp the surface temperature and you need J₀(x) = 0. Let it exchange heat with a fluid instead and the condition becomes

x J₁(x) = Bi · J₀(x)

which panel 1 plots with its roots marked. Sweep Bi and watch them travel: at Bi ≈ 0.01 they sit almost on the zeros of J₁ (an insulated cylinder), and by Bi ≈ 1000 they have arrived at the zeros of J₀ (a clamped surface). Everything in between is a real cylinder with a real heat-transfer coefficient.

At small Bi the first root is x₁ ≈ √(2Bi) — the lumped limit, where the cylinder is nearly isothermal and cools as a single exponential.

Worth knowing before quoting a number

Where the numbers come from

Bessel values come from backward recurrence, which is accurate at every argument and has no series-to-asymptotic join to go wrong; the recurrence identity Jn−1 + Jn+1 = 2nJn/x holds here to about 1e−16. Zeros are bracketed and bisected rather than polished by Newton alone, which is what keeps the higher orders to full precision.

Norms use the closed form ½[J₀(x)² + J₁(x)²], which is exact for both surface conditions — so the coefficients in the Fourier–Bessel sums are not carrying a quadrature error in their denominators.

A uniform initial temperature does not vanish at a clamped surface, so its expansion rings at the rim exactly as a one-dimensional series would. That is genuine Gibbs from genuinely incompatible data, not a defect: raise terms M and the ringing narrows without shrinking. Step t off zero and it is gone.
