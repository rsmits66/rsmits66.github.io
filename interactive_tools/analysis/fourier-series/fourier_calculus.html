Differentiating and Integrating Fourier Series

One operation multiplies the coefficients by n. The other divides by it. That single difference decides everything.
The two operations, written on the coefficients on (−π, π), period 2π, so d/dx cos nx = −n sin nx exactly
term by term, differentiated

Differentiation multiplies by n

(an, bn) ↦ (n bn, −n an)
a0 ↦ 0
Magnitudes are scaled by n, so the spectrum tilts upward and the high harmonics are amplified. The constant term is destroyed outright — which is a problem you can see for yourself with the sawtooth.
term by term, integrated

Integration divides by n

(an, bn) ↦ (−bn/n, an/n)
Magnitudes are scaled by 1/n, so the spectrum tilts downward. Every such series converges better than the one it came from — integration is a smoothing operation, and it essentially never misbehaves.
the term nobody expects

A nonzero mean makes a ramp

∫−πx f = a0x/2 + C + Σ ( an/n · sin nx − bn/n · cos nx )
The antiderivative of the constant a0/2 is a0x/2, which is not periodic. So when a0 ≠ 0 the integral is a straight line plus a periodic wave, and no Fourier series alone can represent it. Two periods are plotted below so you can watch it drift.
The constant is C = a0π/2 + Σn≤N (−1)n bn/n, fixed by requiring the integral to vanish at x = −π.

The asymmetry, in one sentence

Why integration is safe

Integration divides the n-th coefficient by n. Whatever the decay rate of f was, its integral decays one power faster, so the integrated series converges at least as well — and in fact uniformly, even when f has jumps and its own series does not.

Differentiation multiplies by n and so removes a power of decay. Coefficients like 1/n² become 1/n: still summable in the mean-square sense, still a legitimate Fourier series. But coefficients like 1/n become O(1), and a series whose terms do not tend to zero cannot converge anywhere.

That is the whole warning. The safe hypothesis is that f be continuous as a periodic function and piecewise smooth; then term-by-term differentiation gives the Fourier series of f′. A jump anywhere — including at the endpoints — voids it.

The experiment to run

Walk down the smoothness ladder

Hold N = 10 and step through the specimens in order. Watch only the middle panel.

e^(cos x): the derivative series is indistinguishable from f′. x² and |x|: the derivative is a sawtooth or a square wave, recovered correctly but with Gibbs ringing — differentiating a corner produces a jump. x, sgn(x), pulse: the derivative series leaves the frame and keeps going. Raise N and it gets worse, not better.

Then look at the bottom panel through the same sequence. It barely changes: the integral is accurate for every specimen, jumps and all. Integration does not care about smoothness.

Two specimens that form a chain

sgn and |x|

These two are an exact derivative–antiderivative pair: ∫−πx sgn = |x| − π and d|x|/dx = sgn(x). Both have zero mean, so no ramp appears in either direction and the picture is clean.

Select |x| and the middle panel constructs the square wave from the triangle's coefficients −4/(πn²) — multiply by n, get 4/(πn), which is exactly the square wave's expansion. Valid, because |x| is continuous.

Select sgn(x) and try to go the other way. Its true derivative is zero almost everywhere, so every Fourier coefficient of f′ is zero — yet the differentiated series has terms of constant size 4/π. The two disagree completely, which is what "invalid" means here.

The one that catches people out

The sawtooth loses its constant

Take f(x) = x, whose series is Σ 2(−1)n+1 sin nx / n. Its derivative is the constant 1 on the whole open interval — about as tame as a function gets.

Differentiate term by term and you get Σ 2(−1)n+1 cos nx. Every term has size 2, so the series diverges; but notice something worse first. Term-by-term differentiation always sends a0 to 0, so the resulting series has no constant term. The answer we want is a constant. The method cannot produce it even in principle.

The culprit is not the interior — f is perfectly smooth there — but the endpoints, where the periodic extension jumps from π down to −π. Endpoint behaviour again, exactly as in Lecture 3.
