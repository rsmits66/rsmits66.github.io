Sturm–Liouville Eigenvalues and Boundary Conditions

Where the admissible frequencies come from — and why the inner product has a weight in it.
One form, one inner product, one orthogonality theorem six worked examples, each with eigenvalues known in closed form

Every separation of variables in this course has produced the same shape of problem. Put in Sturm–Liouville form it reads

−( p(x) y′ )′ + q(x) y = λ r(x) y,    a < x < b

together with a condition at each end. The three functions do three different jobs: p carries the flux, q shifts the spectrum, and r — the one that is easy to overlook — is the weight in the inner product:

⟨f, g⟩ = ∫ab f(x) g(x) r(x) dx

Eigenfunctions for different λ are orthogonal in that inner product and generally in no other. The proof is two integrations by parts, and the boundary terms it leaves behind are exactly what the endpoint conditions are there to kill.

separated conditions

One condition per end

α₁y(a) + α₂y′(a) = 0
β₁y(b) + β₂y′(b) = 0
Each end is handled on its own. The eigenvalues come out simple — one eigenfunction each, up to scale — and they interlace: between consecutive λ the eigenfunction gains exactly one interior zero.
periodic conditions

The ends are coupled

y(a) = y(b),   y′(a) = y′(b)
Now the two ends talk to each other, and that is not a separated condition at all. The consequence is double eigenvalues: for each n ≥ 1 both cos and sin belong to λ = n², so the eigenspace is two-dimensional. Ordinary Fourier series is this case.
singular endpoints

When p vanishes

p(a) = 0 ⟹ ask only for boundedness
Legendre has p = 1 − x², Bessel has p = x. At such an end the boundary term vanishes by itself, so no condition is needed beyond keeping y finite — and that alone selects the spectrum.
what changes, what does not

Trigonometric is one case

p = r = 1, q = 0 ⟹ sin, cos
Take p or r away from constant and the eigenfunctions stop being trigonometric — Bessel, Legendre, Chebyshev — but every structural fact survives: real eigenvalues, weighted orthogonality, completeness, expansions.

The demonstration to run first

The weight is not decoration

Choose Bessel order 0. Panel 2 shows two Gram matrices side by side, built from exactly the same eigenfunctions. On the left the integral carries the weight r = x and the matrix is diagonal to about 1e−14. On the right the weight is dropped and the matrix is dense, with off-diagonal entries around 0.2.

Those functions are not almost-orthogonal in the wrong inner product; they are simply not orthogonal at all. Orthogonality is a statement about a particular pairing, and the pairing is dictated by r, which in turn comes from the equation.

Chebyshev makes the same point more sharply still, since there the weight 1/√(1−x²) blows up at both ends and does most of its work in a thin layer near the boundary.

Separated against periodic

Where double eigenvalues come from

Switch between the first two problems and watch the ladder in panel 2. With separated conditions every level carries one eigenfunction. With periodic conditions every level above the first carries two, marked in bronze.

The reason is that periodic conditions are not separated: they tie the value and slope at one end to the other rather than constraining each end alone. That leaves a two-parameter family at each λ instead of a one-parameter one.

This is exactly why an ordinary Fourier series needs both a sine and a cosine at every frequency, while a half-range sine series needs only one. The boundary conditions, not the equation, decide how many you get.

Robin, and where frequencies come from

Sweep H and watch λ move

On the trigonometric problem set both ends to Robin and sweep H. At H ≈ 0.01 the eigenvalues sit almost on the Neumann values; by H ≈ 100 they have slid onto the Dirichlet ones. Robin is the one-parameter family joining the two, and the admissible frequencies move continuously along it.

The eigenvalues are no longer (nπ)². They solve a transcendental equation, which is the usual state of affairs — closed forms are the exception.

Now sweep q₀. Every eigenvalue shifts by exactly q₀ and no eigenfunction moves at all, because adding a constant to q adds a constant to λ. Make q₀ negative enough and λ₁ goes below zero — the spectrum is bounded below, but not by zero.

What this page is and is not

Chosen examples, not a solver

Each of the six problems here has eigenvalues available in closed form or as roots of a known function: (nπ)² and its Robin relatives, n², n(n+1), and the zeros of J₀ and J₁. Nothing is discretised into a matrix and diagonalised, so the eigenfunctions you see are the real ones rather than approximations to them.

The price is that you cannot type in your own p, q and r. That is deliberate: a general solver would put a layer of numerical error between you and every statement on this page, and the statements are the point.

The integrals are numerical — Simpson's rule on a graded mesh, with the Chebyshev weight handled by the substitution x = cos θ so its endpoint singularity is removed rather than sampled. Gram entries near 1e−14 are at the arithmetic's floor; they illustrate orthogonality and do not prove it.
