Legendre Polynomials and Spherical Boundary Problems

The interval [−1, 1] and the sphere are the same object, once you write x = cos θ.
From the interval to the sphere, and back unit sphere, no dependence on the azimuthal angle

For a temperature independent of the azimuthal angle, Laplace's equation in spherical coordinates reads

(1/r²)(r²ur)r + (1/r²sinθ)(sinθ·uθ)θ = 0

Separating u = R(r)Θ(θ) and substituting x = cos θ turns the θ-equation into Legendre's equation exactly — the same equation, the same p = 1 − x², that appeared in Lecture 9 as a Sturm–Liouville example with singular endpoints:

[(1 − x²)Θ′]′ + n(n+1)Θ = 0  ⟹  Θ = Pn(cos θ)

and leaves r²R″ + 2rR′ − n(n+1)R = 0, whose solutions are rn and r−(n+1). Boundedness at the centre selects the first. The whole interior problem collapses to one line:

u(r, θ) = Σ cn rn Pn(cos θ),    cn = (2n+1)/2 ∫−11 f(x)Pn(x) dx
the weight, at last, vanishes

x = cos θ absorbs it

dx = −sin θ dθ
Legendre orthogonality on [−1, 1] carries no weight. That is not luck: the spherical area element is sin θ dθ dφ, and the substitution has already swallowed the sin θ. Lecture 11's r dr was the same story in the plane.
boundary to interior

Mode n dies like rn

cnrn at radius r
The interior solution is the boundary expansion with each term damped by rn. Fine angular detail on the surface reaches only a thin shell — the same statement as the sinh ratio in Lecture 7, in a tidier form.
the centre

u(0) = c0 = the average

c0 = ½∫−11 f dx
Every rn with n ≥ 1 vanishes at the origin, so only c0 survives — and c0 is exactly the average of f over the sphere. The mean value property, read straight off the series.
singular endpoints

No condition to impose

p = 1 − x² vanishes at x = ±1
The poles are where p dies, so the Sturm–Liouville boundary term dies with it and boundedness alone selects the spectrum λ = n(n+1). The two poles are not a boundary; they are just points on a sphere.

The hemisphere problem

Stated in full

Solve ∇²u = 0 for r < 1, with u bounded at the origin and no dependence on the azimuthal angle, subject to

u(1, θ) = +1 for 0 ≤ θ < π/2
u(1, θ) = −1 for π/2 < θ ≤ π

Only odd n survive, because the data is odd in x = cos θ, and the series begins

u = (3/2)rP₁ − (7/8)r³P₃ + (11/16)r⁵P₅ − …

Two consequences are worth reading off the picture. The equator θ = π/2 stays at exactly zero for every r, since every surviving Pn(0) = 0. And the centre sits at zero too, which is c₀ — the average of +1 and −1 over equal areas.

What to try next

Watch rn do the filtering

Press Sweep N on the hemisphere. On the surface the partial sum rings at the equator and refuses to settle — genuine Gibbs from a genuine jump. But look at the interior: it barely changes after the first few terms.

The reason is in panel 2 on the right. At r = 0.5, mode 9 is multiplied by 0.5⁹ ≈ 0.002. The boundary detail that the series is struggling with never gets more than a shell's depth inside. A harmonic function is smoother in the interior than its own boundary data, and this is that theorem made arithmetic.

Then switch region to outside. Now the factor is r−(n+1), so the same data decays outward instead, and far away only the monopole c₀/r remains.

The geometry panel

Why the poles look crowded

The right half of panel 1 draws a meridian with equally spaced θ and projects each point onto the vertical axis, which is the x = cos θ map. Equal steps in θ bunch up near x = ±1.

That is exactly why a polynomial in x can represent behaviour near the poles cheaply: a smooth function of θ becomes a function of x that is flat at the ends, and polynomials are good at flat ends. It is also why Pn(±1) = ±1 for every n — the poles are single points, not a boundary to be satisfied.

Choose cos θ as the data and the expansion is one term with no error at all: the data already is P₁. Choose cos²θ and it is exactly two terms, ⅓P₀ + ⅔P₂.

Worth knowing before quoting a number

Where the numbers come from

Coefficients are computed by Simpson's rule on a mesh split at every jump in f, which is what keeps the hemisphere values landing on their exact fractions: the first three come out as 1.5, −0.875 and 0.6875 to thirteen digits. The closed forms are cn = Pn−1(0) − Pn+1(0) for the hemisphere and [Pn−1(x₀) − Pn+1(x₀)]/2 for the cap.

Accuracy does fall away with n, and for an honest reason: Pn has enormous high derivatives near the poles — P₂₃ has a fourth derivative of order 10⁸ there — and Simpson's error carries h⁴ times that. So c₁ through roughly c₉ are correct to machine precision, while c₂₃ is good to about 1e−10. That is the quadrature, not the mathematics, and it is far below anything the displays resolve.

The interior field is the truncated series, so the rim of the cross-section shows SN rather than f. The optional outer ring shows what was actually asked for, and the gap between the two rings is the truncation error — most visible at a jump, where it never shrinks, only narrows.

Outside the sphere the r−(n+1) factors grow without bound as r → 0, so the exterior view is drawn only for r ≥ 1. That is not a numerical limitation but the correct domain of that solution.
