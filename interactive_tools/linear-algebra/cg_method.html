The Conjugate Gradient Method

Solving $Ax = b$ for symmetric positive definite $A \in \R^{N\times N}$ — with a worked example for $N=8$, $n=4$.

1. The setting

We are given a symmetric positive definite matrix $A \in \R^{N\times N}$ (i.e. $A^\top = A$ and $\langle Ax,x\rangle_2 > 0$ for $x\neq 0$) and a vector $b \in \R^N$. We want to find $x \in \R^N$ with

$$ Ax \;=\; b. $$

Because $A$ is SPD, it induces an inner product $\langle u,v\rangle_A := \langle Au,v\rangle_2$ and an associated energy norm $\|x\|_A = \sqrt{\langle x,x\rangle_A}$. The method of conjugate gradients constructs successive approximations $x_n$ that minimise the error in this $A$-norm over the affine spaces $x_0 + \K_n(A,b)$, where

$$ \K_n(A,b) \;=\; \mathrm{span}\bigl\{b,\,Ab,\,A^2 b,\,\ldots,\,A^{n-1}b\bigr\} $$

is the $n$th Krylov subspace. Vectors $d_0,d_1,\ldots,d_{n-1}$ are called $A$-conjugate if $\langle Ad_i, d_j\rangle_2 = 0$ for $i\neq j$.

2. Building $A$-conjugate directions (proof sketch)

Theorem (induction step)

Assume that $d_0 = b,\, d_1,\, \ldots,\, d_{n-1}$ are $A$-conjugate and that $$\mathrm{span}\{d_0,\ldots,d_{n-1}\} \;=\; \K_n(A,b).$$ Since $r_n \in \K_n(A,b)^\perp$, the vectors $d_0, \ldots, d_{n-1},\,-r_n$ are linearly independent whenever $r_n \neq 0$. Performing Gram–Schmidt on this collection with respect to $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_A$ produces

$$ d_n \;=\; -r_n + \sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\frac{\langle Ar_n,d_j\rangle_2}{\langle Ad_j,d_j\rangle_2}\, d_j \;\stackrel{(*)}{=}\; -r_n + \beta_{n-1}\, d_{n-1}. $$

The crucial simplification $(*)$ follows from $A\bigl(\K_{n-1}(A,b)\bigr) \subset \K_n(A,b)$ together with $r_n \in \K_n(A,b)^\perp$: $$ \langle Ar_n, d_j\rangle_2 \;=\; \langle r_n, Ad_j\rangle_2 \;=\; 0,\qquad j=0,1,\ldots,n-2. $$ So all but one of the Gram–Schmidt coefficients vanish, leaving a two-term recursion. By construction the new vectors are $A$-conjugate and $\mathrm{span}\{d_0,\ldots,d_n\} = \K_{n+1}(A,b)$, closing the induction.

This is the structural miracle of CG: although in principle each new direction must be orthogonalised against all previous ones, $A$-conjugacy collapses Gram–Schmidt to a single term, giving an $\mathcal O(N^2)$ per-step cost.

3. The algorithm

Conjugate Gradient method

Step 0. Choose $x_0 \in \R^N$ (typically $x_0 = 0$). Set $r_0 = Ax_0 - b$.

For $n = 0, 1, 2, \ldots$:

  1. If $r_n = 0$, stop; the solution is $x_n$.
  2. Otherwise, build the search direction $$ d_n = \begin{cases} -r_0, & n=0, \\ -r_n + \beta_{n-1}\,d_{n-1}, & n\geq 1, \end{cases}\qquad \beta_{n-1} = \dfrac{\|r_n\|_2^2}{\|r_{n-1}\|_2^2}. $$
  3. Compute the step length and update: $$ \alpha_n = \frac{\|r_n\|_2^2}{\langle Ad_n,\,d_n\rangle_2},\qquad x_{n+1} = x_n + \alpha_n d_n,\qquad r_{n+1} = r_n + \alpha_n A d_n. $$

Convention used here (matching the text): $r_n = Ax_n - b$, so $r_0 = -b$ when $x_0 = 0$. The method terminates in at most $N$ steps in exact arithmetic.

4. The geometric picture: $\phi$, gradients, and CG as smart descent

The whole algorithm becomes intuitive once we recognise that solving $Ax = b$ is the same as minimising a quadratic bowl. Define

$$ \phi(x) \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}\langle Ax,\,x\rangle_2 \,-\, \langle b,\,x\rangle_2. $$

Because $A$ is SPD, $\phi$ is strictly convex with a unique minimiser. Differentiating,

$$ \nabla\phi(x) \;=\; Ax - b. $$

So the gradient vanishes exactly where $Ax = b$, and the residual is the gradient:

$$ r_n \;=\; A x_n - b \;=\; \nabla\phi(x_n). $$

The level sets $\{x : \phi(x) = c\}$ are concentric ellipsoids in $\R^N$, all centred at $x_*$. Their shape is governed by the eigenvalues of $A$: long axes correspond to small eigenvalues (shallow directions of $\phi$), short axes to large eigenvalues (steep directions). When the condition number is large, these ellipsoids become highly elongated — and that is precisely when ordinary steepest descent struggles.

Left: the quadratic $\phi$ as a bowl, with $x_0$ on the wall and $x_*$ at the bottom. Right: a 2D contour view. Compared with steepest descent (gray, zigzagging), CG's $A$-conjugate directions reach $x_*$ in exactly two steps when $N=2$.

Two facts visible in the right panel are central:

  • At $x_1$, the new residual $r_1$ is $2$-orthogonal to $d_0$. This is forced by the line-search step: the directional derivative of $\phi$ along $d_0$ vanishes at $x_1$, i.e. $\langle r_1, d_0\rangle_2 = 0$.
  • If we naively picked $d_1 = -r_1$ (steepest descent), we'd zigzag through the elongated ellipses — each step perpendicular to the last in the standard inner product, but not aimed at $x_*$. CG instead chooses $d_1$ $A$-conjugate to $d_0$, which in the geometry of the level-set ellipsoids is the right notion of "perpendicular" — and aims directly at $x_*$.

5. Meanings of $r_n$, $\alpha_n$, $\beta_n$

$r_n$ — the residual is the gradient of $\phi$

$r_n \;=\; A x_n - b \;=\; \nabla\phi(x_n)$

Literally: how badly the equation $Ax = b$ is currently violated. Geometrically: the steepest-ascent direction of $\phi$ at the current iterate. So $-r_n$ is steepest descent. The norm $\|r_n\|_2$ doubles as a termination criterion — when it hits zero, $x_n = x_*$.

A key identity that drives the whole algorithm: successive residuals are mutually orthogonal in the standard inner product, $$ \langle r_i, r_j\rangle_2 = 0 \qquad (i \neq j), $$ because $r_n \in \K_n(A,b)^\perp$. This is what makes the eventual formula for $\beta_{n-1}$ collapse so cleanly.

$\alpha_n$ — the optimal step length along $d_n$

$\alpha_n \;=\; \dfrac{\|r_n\|_2^2}{\langle Ad_n,\,d_n\rangle_2}$

Once the search direction $d_n$ is fixed, CG asks: how far should we step? The answer is whatever value of $\alpha$ minimises the one-dimensional quadratic $\alpha \mapsto \phi(x_n + \alpha d_n)$. Setting the derivative to zero gives

$$ \alpha_n \;=\; -\frac{\langle r_n, d_n\rangle_2}{\langle A d_n, d_n\rangle_2}. $$

Substituting $d_n = -r_n + \beta_{n-1}d_{n-1}$ and using $\langle r_n, d_{n-1}\rangle_2 = 0$ (line-search optimality from the previous step) collapses the numerator to $-\|r_n\|_2^2$.

Read it as a ratio: directional slope ÷ directional curvature. That is exactly a one-dimensional Newton step — and since $\phi$ is genuinely quadratic, the Newton step lands on the line minimum in one shot. CG is a first-order method that nonetheless takes Newton-optimal steps inside each Krylov subspace.

$\beta_{n-1}$ — the conjugacy correction (a.k.a. momentum)

$\beta_{n-1} \;=\; \dfrac{\|r_n\|_2^2}{\|r_{n-1}\|_2^2}$

The new direction must point downhill (which $-r_n$ does) and be $A$-conjugate to all previous directions (which $-r_n$ alone does not). The fix is to recycle a fraction of the previous direction:

$$ d_n \;=\; -r_n \;+\; \beta_{n-1}\, d_{n-1}. $$

The Gram–Schmidt formula initially gives $\beta_{n-1} = \langle Ar_n, d_{n-1}\rangle_2 / \langle Ad_{n-1}, d_{n-1}\rangle_2$. Using the residual update $r_n - r_{n-1} = \alpha_{n-1} A d_{n-1}$ together with $\langle r_n, r_{n-1}\rangle_2 = 0$ collapses this to the simple ratio of squared residual norms.

Read it as momentum: $\beta_{n-1}$ is the share of last step's direction to carry forward. When residuals shrink fast, $\beta_{n-1}$ is small and CG mostly heads along the fresh gradient. When progress stalls, $\beta_{n-1}$ approaches $1$ and the method leans on inherited momentum. This is exactly the heavy-ball method — but with the momentum coefficient chosen optimally at every step rather than tuned by hand.

Putting it all together

symbolrolegeometric meaning
$r_n$ residual / gradient of $\phi$ at $x_n$ which way is steepest uphill, and how far from optimal we are
$\alpha_n$ optimal step length along $d_n$ Newton step in 1D: slope ÷ curvature, lands at the line minimum
$\beta_{n-1}$ conjugacy correction (momentum) how much of $d_{n-1}$ to keep so $d_n$ stays $A$-orthogonal to history
$d_n$ search direction $-\,$gradient $+\,$momentum, mixed so $\phi$ is minimised over all of $\K_{n+1}(A,b)$

A single iteration, in plain words:

  1. Look at the current gradient: $r_n = A x_n - b$.
  2. Mix it with last step's direction using $\beta_{n-1}$:   $d_n = -r_n + \beta_{n-1}\,d_{n-1}$.
  3. Take a Newton-optimal step of length $\alpha_n$ along $d_n$.
  4. Update $x_n \to x_{n+1}$, $r_n \to r_{n+1}$, repeat.

The deep fact is that step 2 — even though it only orthogonalises against the previous direction — produces a $d_n$ that is automatically $A$-conjugate to every prior direction. That is the gift symmetry of $A$ gives you, and it is why each Newton-optimal line search along $d_n$ amounts to minimising $\phi$ over the full Krylov subspace $\K_{n+1}(A,b)$ rather than just the line.

6. Worked example: $N=8$, $n=4$

We pick the classical SPD matrix — the 1D discrete Laplacian $A = \mathrm{tridiag}(-1,\,2,\,-1) \in \R^{8\times 8}$, with right-hand side $b = (1,1,\ldots,1)^\top$. This matrix is SPD because $A = L^\top L$ where $L$ is bidiagonal (it is also strictly diagonally dominant on the boundary rows with positive diagonal).

7. Why convergence here is exact in 4 steps

For this particular $b = (1,\ldots,1)^\top$, the residuals stay in the symmetric-about-the-middle subspace of $\R^8$, which is invariant under $A$ and only $4$-dimensional. Hence the Krylov sequence terminates at $\K_4(A,b)$, and CG — being optimal in the energy norm over $\K_n(A,b)$ — produces the exact solution at step $n=4$.

The exact solution can also be derived from the 1D Poisson finite-difference formula $x_i = \tfrac{i\,(N+1-i)}{2}$, giving $(4,7,9,10,10,9,7,4)^\top$, which matches $x_4$ computed above.
