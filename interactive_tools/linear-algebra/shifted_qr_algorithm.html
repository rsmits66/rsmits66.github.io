The Shifted QR Algorithm

The standard method for finding all eigenvalues of a dense matrix. Enter a 4×4 matrix, step through iterations to watch the subdiagonals collapse, then let the computer finish.

1. The Algorithm

Reduce $A$ to upper Hessenberg form first, then iterate shifted QR until the subdiagonals decay.

Setup: Reduce $A_0 = A$ to upper Hessenberg form $H_0 = Q_0^\top A_0 Q_0$ via two Householder similarity transforms. Eigenvalues are preserved; the matrix now has zeros below the subdiagonal.

Iterate for $k = 0, 1, 2, \ldots$:

  1. Choose shift $\mu_k$ — Wilkinson shift, the eigenvalue of the bottom-right $2 \times 2$ block of $H_k$ closer to $(H_k)_{nn}$.
  2. Factor $H_k - \mu_k I = Q_k R_k$ via Householder reflectors.
  3. Reassemble $H_{k+1} = R_k Q_k + \mu_k I$ — preserves Hessenberg structure.
  4. Deflate when a subdiagonal entry becomes negligible. Lock the eigenvalue and shrink the active block.

Each step is a similarity: $H_{k+1} = Q_k^\top H_k Q_k$. Subdiagonals shrink cubically (symmetric case) or quadratically (general case).

3. Reading the Display

Cells are color-coded by role:

Purple: diagonal (eigenvalue estimates)
Red: subdiagonal > 0.1
Orange: subdiagonal 0.01 – 0.1
Yellow: subdiagonal 1e-6 – 0.01
Green: subdiagonal < 1e-6 (converged)

When a subdiagonal goes green, the matrix block-decouples at that row. We lock in the eigenvalue (or a $2 \times 2$ complex-conjugate block) and continue on the smaller active region — deflation.

4. Practical Notes

  • Hessenberg reduction first. Without it, each QR step costs $O(n^3)$. After reduction to Hessenberg (or tridiagonal for symmetric $A$), each step is $O(n^2)$ — the algorithm's key optimization.
  • Implicit shifts (bulge chasing). Production implementations never form $A - \mu I$ explicitly. They introduce a "bulge" via the first column of $(A - \mu_1 I)(A - \mu_2 I)$ and chase it down the Hessenberg form, supporting complex shifts in real arithmetic via Francis double-shifts.
  • Wilkinson shift. Choosing $\mu_k$ as the bottom-2×2 eigenvalue closer to $(A_k)_{nn}$ guarantees convergence and gives cubic rates for symmetric matrices. The naïve Rayleigh shift $\mu_k = (A_k)_{nn}$ can fail on some matrices.
  • Real Schur form. Complex eigenvalues of real matrices appear as $2 \times 2$ diagonal blocks. Trace and determinant of the block recover the conjugate pair, all in real arithmetic.
  • Connection to RQI. The Wilkinson shift is essentially a Rayleigh quotient on the bottom rows. Each QR step is one Rayleigh Quotient Iteration applied simultaneously to all eigenpairs. That's why the convergence rates match.
